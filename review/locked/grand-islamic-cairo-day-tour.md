## Opening

Most visitors come for the pyramids and see Islamic Cairo as a minaret from the taxi window. This private day corrects that: seven sites across roughly a thousand years, from the Mosque of Ibn Tulun and the collector’s house beside it to the Citadel, Sultan Hassan and the Rifai Mosque, an Ottoman merchant’s house on Al-Muizz Street and the Khan El Khalili bazaar. Each gets enough time for the history to make sense rather than blur.

---

## Why the day runs this way

It starts with Ibn Tulun’s great courtyard and, next door, a house small and human enough to wander slowly, before the monumental architecture takes over. The Citadel and the two great mosques below it fill the middle of the day; the afternoon moves north into the medieval streets, to see how a wealthy family actually lived, and ends at the bazaar. Because the day is yours alone, the order can bend: a longer hour at Sultan Hassan, a shorter one at the Citadel, the bazaar saved for the last light.

## Your day

### Morning · Ibn Tulun, Gayer-Anderson and the Citadel

**Pickup from your hotel**
Your driver and Egyptologist collect you in a private air-conditioned car.

**The Mosque of Ibn Tulun**
Built in the 9th century by Ahmad ibn Tulun, governor of Egypt, around a vast open courtyard, with a minaret whose stair spirals up the outside. A short stop, before the house next door.
*Sites:* Mosque of Ibn Tulun

**The Gayer-Anderson Museum**
Two 16th- and 17th-century townhouses joined by a bridge, beside the Mosque of Ibn Tulun, where the British officer and collector Robert Gayer-Anderson lived from 1935 to 1942. They’re kept much as he left them: Pharaonic bronzes, Persian carpets, inlaid furniture, light through mashrabiya screens, and a roof terrace looking onto Ibn Tulun’s minaret.
*Sites:* Gayer-Anderson Museum

**The Citadel and the Mosque of Muhammad Ali**
Begun by Saladin in 1176, and the seat of Egyptian power for nearly 700 years. Its mosque, completed in 1848, borrows from Istanbul’s imperial mosques: twin minarets, cascading half-domes, alabaster walls, and a clock tower given by King Louis-Philippe that reportedly never kept good time. The smaller museums inside are optional, as your interest takes you.
*Sites:* Citadel of Saladin · Mosque of Muhammad Ali

### Midday · Sultan Hassan, the Rifai Mosque and lunch

**Sultan Hassan and the Rifai Mosque**
Sultan Hassan, begun in 1356, is mosque, school and mausoleum in one, its four great halls for the four Sunni schools of law, its portal about 38 metres high. The sultan was never buried in his tomb. Facing it, the Rifai Mosque, finished in 1912 to echo its neighbour, holds King Farouk and the last Shah of Iran.
*Sites:* Sultan Hassan Mosque-Madrasa · Rifai Mosque

**Lunch at a local restaurant (included)**
Drinks aren’t included.

### Afternoon · Al-Muizz Street and the bazaar

**Beit el-Suhaymi**
A wealthy merchant’s house, put together in the 17th and early 18th centuries, read like a plan once it’s explained: public reception rooms below, private family quarters above behind carved screens, and a tree-shaded courtyard cooling the house in a city of 38°C summers.
*Sites:* Beit el-Suhaymi · Al-Muizz Street

**Khan El Khalili**
The bazaar grew around a caravanserai founded in 1382. Leave the main silver-and-perfume lanes and it turns exploratory: spice merchants, coppersmiths, cloth wholesalers. Take tea near El Fishawy and spend the time as you like.
*Sites:* Khan El Khalili
