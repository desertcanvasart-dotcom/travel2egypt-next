# Egypt’s Sound and Light Shows: Which to See, and What the Evening Involves

Egypt’s sound and light shows are not modern spectacles. Most are older productions: recorded narration, music, and coloured light on stone. If you arrive expecting the projection mapping of a new city festival, you will be disappointed at most of them, and visitor reviews say so regularly.

What they offer is something else. They are the only ordinary way to be inside these monuments at night. The crowds have gone, the heat has gone, and the columns at Karnak or the façade at Abu Simbel are lit against a black sky. For many visitors that is worth the ticket, whatever they think of the script.

So the useful question is not which show is best. It is which evening suits you. And the first thing that decides that is whether you sit or walk.

## Sit or walk: the difference that matters most

| Show | What you do | Why people go | The main thing to plan for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Giza Pyramids** | Sit outdoors, facing the Sphinx and pyramids | The pyramids and Sphinx lit together | Cairo traffic, and finding the evening entrance |
| **Karnak, Luxor** | Walk and stand through the temple, then sit by the Sacred Lake | Being among the lit columns | Uneven ground in the dark, and standing time |
| **Philae, Aswan** | Cross by boat, walk and stand, then sit for the finale | Arriving at a lit island by water | Boats, boarding, and the language of the night |
| **Abu Simbel** | Sit facing the two temples | The façades and the projections on them | Wind off the lake, and a dark walk out |
| **Edfu** | Move between parts of the temple | Projections on columns, carvings and pylons | Little reliable current detail |
| **Qaitbay, Alexandria** | Sit inside the fortress walls | Alexandria’s story on a medieval citadel | Coastal weather |

One caution applies to all six. Seated does not mean step-free. The approach, the seating tiers, the toilets and the way out can each be harder than the show itself.

## The show is not the whole evening

A forty-five-minute seated show sounds easy. The evening around it may not be.

At Giza there is the drive through Cairo traffic. At Philae there are two boat crossings and the wait for each. At Abu Simbel there is a walk out in the dark over rough ground. Someone who is comfortable sitting for an hour may still struggle with the queue, the boarding or the exit.

When you weigh a show, weigh the whole evening: pickup, approach, performance, exit and the drive back.

## Giza: a seated view of the pyramids

You sit in an outdoor viewing area facing the Sphinx, with the three pyramids behind. The Sphinx narrates, and the story covers the kings who built them: Khufu, Khafre and Menkaure. It is usually described as lasting forty-five minutes to an hour. You do not walk through the plateau. This is one fixed view.

What people like is the place. Seeing the pyramids after dark, at the end of a Cairo visit, is the point. What people complain about is the production: dated narration, modest effects, quiet stretches, and a gap between the advertising and the evening. Some recent reviews also mention sound quality and poor value in the VIP seats. Those are individual reports, not a verdict on every night.

Three practical points. First, the show is running, but confirm your own date. A performance depends on audience numbers, and the operator’s page currently shows no usable timetable. Second, get the evening entrance and meeting point specifically. It is in the village below the Sphinx, not at the daytime plateau gate. Third, allow generously for traffic, and agree where your driver will collect you.

Separately, a virtual-reality version was announced for Giza in 2025. It is a different product. Make sure you know which one you are booking.

## Karnak: the walk is the show

At Karnak you move. The audience is led through the temple while recorded voices, music and light introduce each space in turn: the avenue of sphinxes, the first court, the great columned hall. The evening ends seated, on tiers overlooking the Sacred Lake.

Operators describe the whole thing as taking an hour to seventy-five minutes. One describes thirty to forty minutes of walking and standing before the seated part. Treat those as planning estimates.

The appeal is scale. Passing between lit columns that are taller than a house is a different thing from watching a façade from a seat, and Egypt’s own tourism authority promotes the night walk as the attraction. Visitors praise the atmosphere. Others find the narration ponderous, or the lighting less dramatic than they hoped.

The warnings are physical. The ground is uneven stone and sand, in low light. There is real standing time, and standing still is often harder on knees and backs than walking. You move at the show’s pace, not your own, so this is not the moment to study carvings. And people who were warm while walking get cold quickly once they sit by the lake.

**For us:** the strongest evening of the six for anyone who is steady on their feet. It needs a clear warning for anyone who is not. If someone in your party cannot manage the walk, they can be taken straight to the seating by the Sacred Lake. Say so when you book. ## Philae: a boat, a walk and a seat

Philae is on an island, so the evening begins on the water. The temple appears lit from the boat before anything else happens, and for many people that arrival is the best moment of the night.

It is not a seated show. A detailed first-hand account describes the sequence: landing, standing by the colonnade, moving through the courtyard and the Temple of Isis, walking beside the temple, and then benches for the finale, looking toward Trajan’s Kiosk. The same writer loved the atmosphere and felt hurried past places where she wanted to stop.

The performance takes about an hour. The evening takes a good deal longer, with the drive, the boats and the waiting.

Opinions divide. Some visitors prefer Philae to Karnak. Another travel diary records a flat evening after an exhausting day, which says as much about the day as about the show. Do not schedule Philae on the night you return from Abu Simbel.

What to settle before you go: whether your price covers both boat crossings, the waiting time and the transfers, since a show ticket alone does not. When and where the return boat leaves. Whether everyone can manage stepping on and off a small boat in the dark, which is a separate question from the walking. And which language the show will be in.

**For us:** the most atmospheric of the six, and the one that needs the most arranging. We arrange it as [an evening transfer with both boats and entrance](https://travel2egypt.org/private-tour-sound-light-show-at-philae-temple).

## Abu Simbel: seated, exposed and remote

You sit on raised seating facing the two temples. Projections move across the façades, and the story covers Ramesses II, Nefertari and the rescue of the temples from the rising lake in the 1960s.

Here the setting draws more agreement than the production. Some visitors call the lit temples the highlight of their trip. Others describe the show as dated. The production is not the reason to go. The temples are.

The winter conditions are well documented. One visitor describes concrete seats, their back to Lake Nasser, a strong wind, and a dark uphill walk out over dirt and rock. That is one account, and a useful one. Bring a windproof jacket over a warm layer, long trousers and closed shoes. A small seat pad helps. Bring a torch for the way out.

Two things decide whether you can see it at all. You have to be in Abu Simbel in the evening, which means staying overnight or arriving on a [Lake Nasser cruise](https://travel2egypt.org/blog/lake-nasser-cruise), whose guests watch the show during their stop. The usual morning trip from Aswan leaves long before dark. And the show needs an audience: the operator states a minimum of ten foreign visitors, or fifty Egyptian visitors, for the first performance.

**For us:** the most iconic of the six. If you are in Abu Simbel for the night, go.

## Edfu: the one we can say least about

Edfu has a show, and it is a moving one. Production material describes the audience shifting between scenes: an opening section, action in the colonnaded court, and a final act in front of the temple. So it should not be sold as seated.

A projector manufacturer’s case study confirms that the columns and the carved pylons are used as projection surfaces. It gives the running time as thirty-seven minutes. Other material says fifty. Somewhere between forty and fifty minutes is the honest estimate.

Edfu’s advantage is the building. It is among the best-preserved temples in Egypt, so there is a great deal of intact surface to project on. But there is very little dependable recent visitor feedback, and we will not invent a consensus.

The practical obstacle is simple. Most people see Edfu from a Nile cruise, in the morning, and sail on. To see the show, your boat has to be moored at Edfu through the evening. Some cruises do stay, and their guests are the audience. Others sail on. Ask before you count on it.

## Qaitbay, Alexandria: the newest, and a different story

The show at the Citadel of Qaitbay opened in October 2024, which makes it far newer than the temple shows. It is seated, inside the fortress walls, and it tells Alexandria’s story and not a pharaoh’s: Alexander’s founding of the city, the Lighthouse, the Library, Cleopatra and Caesar, and the Islamic centuries that built the fort itself.

Newer does not automatically mean better. The handful of reviews that are about the show itself praise the organisation and the picture and sound quality. One enjoyed the atmosphere and found the history thin. That is too few voices for a firm claim.

The fortress stands on the open harbour, so wind is the issue. Check the forecast, bring a windproof layer, and do not assume the ticket includes time to explore the citadel or climb its towers.

**For us:** a very pleasant evening, and at its best in summer, when the sea air is welcome.

## Languages and headsets

The shows run in several languages, and the operator advertises a choice of ten. How you get yours depends on the venue and the night.

At some shows, one language plays on the main speakers and the others come through a headset. At Karnak the first show of the evening is in English. At Philae the language follows the day of the week, and on some evenings both performances are in French and Spanish only on the speakers. At Qaitbay, a visitor without a guide needs a passport to borrow a translation device.

Ask which language is on the speakers for your date and time. If you need a headset, collect it before the start, while someone is still there to help.

## Will the show actually run?

Not always. This is the most common surprise.

The shows have minimum audiences. At Giza, Karnak and Philae the second performance of the evening needs at least five foreign visitors. At Edfu and Qaitbay it is six. At Abu Simbel even the first show needs ten. In a quiet week, a show can be cancelled on the night.

The operator says that tickets bought through its own website have priority and are honoured regardless. Its published schedules also carry a standing note that dates are subject to change. So confirm on the day, not the week before, and do not build a journey around a show without a second plan for the evening.

## What to wear after dark

Warm days are a poor guide. Egyptian winter nights are cold when you are sitting still outdoors, and three of these venues are exposed: Abu Simbel to the lake wind, Philae on the boat crossing, Giza on the open desert edge. A visitor at Giza in February reported a cold, breezy evening.

From November to March, bring layers, a windproof outer jacket, long trousers and closed shoes. Add a scarf or hat if you feel the cold. Flat shoes with grip matter at Karnak, Philae and Edfu. A small torch helps on the way in and out. Keep it pointed at the ground and away from the projections.

## How to read the reviews

Carefully. Many star ratings on booking sites are ratings of the tour company, not of the show. One Qaitbay listing displays hundreds of reviews for the operator while stating that the experience itself has not yet been reviewed. Praise for a driver tells you the driver was good.

Read for specifics. A review that mentions cold seats, a broken headset or a long standing section is telling you something you can prepare for. A review that says only “magical” or “boring” is telling you about the reviewer’s expectations.

## If you see only one

Nobody needs to see more than one or two. The scripts overlap, and the format is the same idea each time.

For us, having seen all six, Abu Simbel is the most iconic. If you will be there overnight, that is the one. If you want to walk through a temple at night, choose Karnak. If you want atmosphere, choose Philae. If you want the pyramids in the dark from a seat, choose Giza, and expect the view to be better than the show. In Alexandria, Qaitbay is an easy evening, at its best in summer, and the only one with a different story to tell.

And if none of this appeals, skip them. An evening walk along the Luxor corniche, with the temple lit beside the road, costs nothing.

## What to confirm before booking

- **That the show is running** on your date, and at which time.
- **The language** on the main speakers, and how to get a headset in yours.
- **What the price covers:** ticket only, or transfers and, at Philae, both boats.
- **How much walking and standing** there is, and whether there is a direct route to the seats.
- **The meeting point and the pickup point** for the evening, which may differ from the daytime entrance.
- **The cancellation terms** if the minimum audience is not reached.
- **Toilets and water** before the start, and whether you can leave partway through.

## Who it suits, and who it doesn’t

These shows suit visitors who want to see a monument at night and are happy with an old-fashioned telling of its story. They suit a free evening in Luxor or Aswan particularly well, when there is little else on after dark.

They do not suit anyone expecting a modern light show. We do not recommend them for small children: the narration is long and the evening is late. And the walking shows do not suit anyone who is unsteady on rough ground in the dark, however short the distance sounds.

## In sum

Decide first whether you want to sit or walk. Then count the whole evening, not just the performance. Confirm the show is running, find out which language you will hear, and dress for a cold night.

Go for the monument, not the script. The stone at night is the part people remember.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which is the best sound and light show in Egypt?

Reviews of every show are mixed. For us, Abu Simbel is the most iconic. Karnak is the most immersive because you walk through the temple. Philae has the strongest atmosphere. Giza has the most famous view.

### Is the sound and light show at the pyramids worth it?

It is worth it for the sight of the pyramids and Sphinx lit at night. The production is dated, and visitors expecting modern effects are often disappointed. Confirm your date before you book.

### Do you walk or sit at the Karnak sound and light show?

Both. You walk and stand through the temple for the first part, on uneven ground in low light, and then sit by the Sacred Lake for the finale.

### Is the Philae sound and light show seated?

Only at the end. You cross to the island by boat, walk and stand through the temple, and sit on benches for the finale.

### Are the sound and light shows in English?

Often, but not always on the main speakers. The language depends on the venue, the day and the performance, and other languages are provided by headset. Ask about your specific date.

### Can a sound and light show be cancelled?

Yes. The shows have minimum audience numbers, and a performance can be cancelled if too few people attend. Confirm on the day.

### What should you wear to a sound and light show in winter?

Layers, a windproof jacket, long trousers and closed shoes. You will be sitting still outdoors at night, and Abu Simbel, Philae and Giza are all exposed to wind.
