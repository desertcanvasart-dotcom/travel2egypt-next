# The Fayoum Portraits: The Faces of Roman Egypt, and Where to See Them

The Fayoum portraits are not portraits in the sense a gallery uses the word. Each one is a piece of a mummy. It was painted on a thin wooden panel, laid over the face of an embalmed body, and bound in place with linen. Then it was buried, and was not meant to be seen again.

In a museum today, most hang alone on a wall at eye level, framed and lit like any other painting. That is how they are easiest to love. It is also how they are easiest to misread.

There are about a thousand of them in collections today. Most were made in Egypt between the first and the third centuries AD, under Roman rule, and a few are later. They are among the most substantial surviving groups of painted portraits from antiquity. Nearly everything else of the kind, from Greece and from Rome itself, has rotted away. These survived because Egypt is dry and because they were underground.

## What a mummy portrait actually is

Picture the whole object, not the panel. A body, mummified in the traditional Egyptian way. Linen wrappings, often folded into a deep diamond pattern with a gilded stud at the centre of each diamond. And at the head, where an older Egyptian mummy would have a moulded mask, a flat painting of a living-looking face, set into the bandages like a window.

A few museums still show complete portrait mummies, and if you have the chance, start with one. The effect is stranger and more moving than any detached panel. The painting is Roman. The body is Egyptian. Neither tradition gave way.

Not every example is on wood. Some are painted on linen shrouds that wrapped the whole body. And a portrait was never the only choice. In one family burial at Hawara, known as the Tomb of Aline, a mother and two children had painted portraits while the husband had a gilded mask. These were the dead of families who could pay.

## Why “Fayoum” is only half right

The name comes from two finds in the 1880s, both in the Fayoum, the large oasis south-west of Cairo. One was at Hawara. The other was at er-Rubayat, the cemetery of the ancient town of Philadelphia. Together they put hundreds of portraits on the market and into museums within a few years, and the label stuck.

But the same kind of portrait has been found at Saqqara and Memphis, at Thebes, at Akhmim, on the north coast, and above all at Antinoopolis in Middle Egypt, a city the emperor Hadrian founded in AD 130. Scholars now prefer “mummy portraits”. “Fayoum portraits” remains the name most people know, and it does no harm if you remember that it describes a type, not an address.

## Who these people were

This is where captions tend to say too much.

The names written on some portraits and mummies are mostly Greek: Artemidorus, Hermione, Eutyches, Demetrios. Their clothes, jewellery and hairstyles follow the fashions of the Roman court closely enough that hair is one clue to dating a portrait, alongside where and how it was found. Their burials are Egyptian in every religious detail, with Anubis, Osiris and Isis on the wrappings.

So they were people of a mixed provincial society, descended in part from Greek settlers of the centuries after Alexander, living under Roman law and buried by Egyptian rite. Beyond that, be cautious. You cannot read ancestry from a painted face, and attempts to do so tell you more about the person looking than the person painted. The portraits show how these families chose to present their dead. It is better evidence for fashion and belief than for descent.

Who they were as individuals is almost entirely lost. For most there is no name. For a handful there is a name and one more fact. A portrait with a biography attached is nearly always a portrait with an invented one.

## How they were painted

There were two main methods.

**Encaustic** is pigment mixed into beeswax. It could be applied warm with a brush or worked with a small metal tool, and it produces thick, glossy, sculpted paint. The portraits people remember are mostly encaustic: the wet-looking eyes, the highlight on the lower lip, the flush in the cheek.

**Tempera** is pigment bound with animal glue. It dries matte and quick, and suits flatter, more linear painting. Tempera portraits can look plainer on a first glance and reward a second.

Many tested panels are imported limewood, from a tree that does not grow in Egypt, and some are only a few millimetres thick. Others are native sycamore fig, and some boards from Antinoopolis are as thick as 2 centimetres. Thin wood bends, which mattered where the panel had to curve slightly over a face.

Gold leaf was added for wreaths, jewellery and sometimes the whole background.

## Painted from life, or after death?

For a long time the accepted story was that these were painted during the sitter’s lifetime, hung in the house, and reused for the mummy at death. It is a pleasing idea and you will still see it stated as fact.

The evidence is mixed. Some panels were cut across the upper corners to fit the wrappings, which suggests they started as rectangles. One portrait from Hawara was found still in a wooden frame with a cord for hanging. So at least one was made to hang. Where it hung, and for how long, is not known.

On the other side, scans of intact portrait mummies generally find that the age and sex of the body match the face. The portraits show a great many young adults and children because a great many people died young, not because everyone was painted at twenty. But a scan can only compare a body with its portrait. It cannot say when the sitting took place. And when a portrait was painted is a separate question from when it was cut down and bound into the wrappings.

The fair summary is that practice probably varied. Some were painted in life. Many were painted at or after death. No single rule covers a thousand paintings made over two hundred years.

## How to look at one

Give a single portrait five minutes. This is an order that works.

**The eyes first.** They are usually larger than life, set slightly too high, with a white highlight placed in each. The enlargement is deliberate. This is a face meant to be met.

**Then the direction of the light.** In the best encaustic portraits it falls from one side, and the painter has followed it across the forehead, down the nose and onto the neck. The turn of a head, a bright stroke on the nose and the shadow under the chin give these small panels a striking sense of presence.

**Then the hair and the jewellery.** A woman’s tight curls piled at the front, or a braided bun, point to a particular reign. Pearl earrings, emerald necklaces and gold chains are painted from real types found in Roman graves.

**Then the gold.** Look for a wreath added over the finished hair. It was often applied last, as part of preparing the dead, and it sits on top of the paint.

**Last, the edges.** Find the cut corners. Find the darker band round the margin where resin and linen once overlapped the wood. This is where the mummy was. It is the part of the painting that tells you what it was for.

## Five to know

**Artemidorus**, British Museum (EA 21810). A complete portrait mummy from Hawara, of the early second century. The case is red, covered in Egyptian funerary scenes in gold leaf. Across its chest, below the painted panel, is a Greek farewell with a spelling mistake: “Farewell, Artemidorus.” A young man, a Greek name, Egyptian gods, a Roman-style face. If you see one portrait mummy in your life, this is a good one.

**Hermione**, Girton College, Cambridge. Found at Hawara in Petrie’s 1910–11 season, and at Girton since 1911. The inscription calls her *grammatike*. It has been translated as “literary lady” and as “language teacher”. If the second is right, she is one of the few named working women of antiquity. The word is all there is.

**Eutyches**, Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York (18.9.2). A boy in a white tunic, painted between AD 100 and 150, with a Greek inscription naming him as the freedman of a man called Kasanios. A child who had been a slave, and was given a portrait.

**The Tondo of the Two Brothers**, Egyptian Museum, Cairo (CG 33267). A round painting in encaustic of two young men side by side, from Sheikh Abadeh, the ancient Antinoopolis, of the mid-second century. The title is conventional. It is not known that they were brothers. It was restored in 2026, shown at Tahrir on 11 October, and has left Egypt for the first time, for an exhibition in Rome.

**Herakleides**, Getty Villa, Los Angeles (91.AP.6). An intact portrait mummy of a young man, of AD 120–140. When it was scanned, a mummified ibis was found inside the wrappings, laid on his body. Nobody is certain why.

## How they left Egypt

In 1887 people digging near er-Rubayat began finding portraits. An Austrian dealer, Theodor Graf, bought them in quantity, toured them round Europe and America, and sold them one by one. Almost none has a recorded grave, a body, or a companion object.

At Hawara, in 1888–89 and again in 1910–11, Flinders Petrie excavated with more care than anyone before him and recorded what he found. He also separated most of the portraits from their mummies, kept the paintings, and left the bodies. His finds were divided between Cairo and his sponsors and went on to museums in several countries.

The result is that the portraits are among the best-loved objects from ancient Egypt and among the worst documented. For most of them, the painting is the only evidence that the person existed.

## What the laboratories are finding

Since 2013 the Getty Museum has run a project called APPEAR, in which museums pool technical studies of their portraits: which wood, which pigments, which binders, which tools. The Getty lists more than sixty participating institutions.

The results are filling in what the excavators lost. Repeated choices of material, ways of handling paint and small visual habits let scholars propose groups of related works, and behind them, workshops. These are hypotheses, and are published as such. In the portraits studied so far, purples were made by mixing indigo with madder, and greens from indigo and a yellow arsenic mineral, a recipe now pushed back centuries earlier than had been thought. The findings are free to read in [volume 1](https://www.getty.edu/publications/mummyportraits/) and [volume 2](https://www.getty.edu/publications/mummy-portraits-2/), the second published in 2026.

Science will not recover the names. It is beginning to recover the painters.

## Where to see them

### In Egypt

The **Egyptian Museum in Tahrir**, Cairo, holds the country’s principal collection, and its visitor brochure marks a Fayoum portraits section.

There is a timing issue. Egypt has lent portraits abroad for the first time, to an exhibition at the Ara Pacis Museum in Rome that runs from 5 November 2026 to 2 May 2027. The loan is not one painting. It includes the Tondo of the Two Brothers, encaustic portraits from Hawara and a portrait on linen, and the organisers describe it as nearly all the Fayoum panels in Egyptian collections. If the portraits are the reason for your visit to Tahrir in that period, ask the museum which examples are on display before you go.

In Alexandria, the **Antiquities Museum of the Bibliotheca Alexandrina** lists a Fayoum portrait in showcase 34, in its hall on the afterlife. It is a different museum from the city’s Graeco-Roman Museum.

### Abroad

- **British Museum**, London. Artemidorus and other Hawara portraits.
- **Petrie Museum**, University College London. A large group from Petrie’s own excavations, shown with the archaeology.
- **Manchester Museum**. Portraits and portrait mummies from Hawara.
- **Metropolitan Museum of Art**, New York.
- **Getty Villa**, Los Angeles. Herakleides, and the home of the research project.
- **Louvre**, Paris, including examples from Antinoopolis.
- **State museums of Berlin**, including the family group known as the Tomb of Aline.

Displays rotate. These paintings are fragile and light-sensitive, and any museum may have fewer on view than it owns.

### In Rome, until May 2027

The Ara Pacis exhibition brings together nearly fifty portraits within more than 110 works. Egypt’s own contribution is given as 24 portraits and 16 other objects. For anyone in Europe this winter it is a rare chance to see so many together.

## Seeing where they came from

You can stand in the cemetery where the Hawara portraits were found. It lies beside the pyramid of Amenemhat III in the Fayoum, about two hours from Cairo.

There is very little to see: low mounds, sand, the pyramid behind. No portrait remains there. But it is the one place where the paintings reconnect with a landscape, and it sits beside one of the strangest archaeological sites in the country. That site is the subject of [Egypt’s Lost Labyrinth at Hawara](https://travel2egypt.org/blog/hawara-labyrinth).

The pyramid is Middle Kingdom. The portraits are Roman, the better part of two thousand years later. The families who buried their dead here were choosing ground that was already ancient.

## What to confirm before you go

- **What is on display.** Ask the museum, or check its website, in the week of your visit. This applies to Cairo most of all until the Rome loan returns.
- **Photography.** Rules differ by museum and sometimes by room. Flash is banned everywhere.
- **The Petrie Museum’s hours.** It is a university museum with shorter opening times than the national collections.
- **Access at Hawara.** The site is open, but areas may be closed if excavation is under way.

## In sum

Look at the face, because the painter meant you to. Then look at the edges, because that is where the truth of the object is.

These were not made as art and not made for us. They were made so that a particular person would be recognised after death. Two thousand years on, with the names gone and the bodies mostly lost, that is the one thing they still do.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are the Fayoum portraits?

They are painted portraits of the dead, made in Roman Egypt, mainly between the first and third centuries AD. Each was placed over the face of a mummy. About a thousand survive.

### Where can you see Fayoum portraits in Egypt?

Mainly at the Egyptian Museum in Tahrir, Cairo, though much of its collection is on loan to Rome until 2 May 2027. The Bibliotheca Alexandrina’s Antiquities Museum in Alexandria also lists one. Check what is on display before you visit.

### Why are they called Fayoum portraits?

Because the first large groups were found in the Fayoum in the 1880s, at Hawara and er-Rubayat. Similar portraits have since been found across Egypt, and “mummy portraits” is the more accurate term.

### Were the Fayoum portraits painted while the person was alive?

Some probably were, and many probably were not. The evidence points both ways, and practice is likely to have varied over two centuries.

### How were the Fayoum portraits made?

Most are painted on thin wooden panels in encaustic, which is pigment in beeswax, or in tempera, which is pigment in animal glue. Some are painted on linen shrouds.

### Who were the people in the Fayoum portraits?

Well-off members of a mixed society in Roman Egypt, many with Greek names, dressed in Roman fashion and buried by Egyptian rite. Very few can be identified as individuals.
