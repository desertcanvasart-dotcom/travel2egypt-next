# Egypt’s Lost Labyrinth at Hawara: What Survives, and What Was Claimed

There is no labyrinth to walk through at Hawara. There is a dark, weathered pyramid of mudbrick, a modern canal, and a wide stretch of sand and limestone chips with a few broken columns lying in it. A visitor who arrives expecting corridors will be standing in a field.

And yet the ancient world agreed that the greatest building in Egypt stood here. Not at Giza. Here. Herodotus said so plainly, and for five centuries other writers repeated it.

Both things are true, and the distance between them is the reason to come. Hawara is a place where you have to weigh evidence: what was written, what was dug up, what a scan suggests, and what someone on the internet would like to be true.

## What you actually see

The pyramid first. It is a hill of grey-black mudbrick, rounded by weather, with none of the clean geometry of Giza. It once stood around 58 metres tall and was cased in white limestone. The casing was stripped in antiquity, and what remains is the core.

South of the pyramid is the area where the Labyrinth stood. You will see a broad, uneven surface of sand and stone fragments, pieces of granite and limestone column, and some exposed blocks. An irrigation canal cuts straight across it.

North of the pyramid is the cemetery. To the eye it is low mounds and pits. It is also the source of some of the most famous paintings to survive from the ancient world.

That is the site. It takes about an hour to walk. What follows is what makes the hour worthwhile.

## The king who built twice

The pyramid belongs to Amenemhat III, a king of the Twelfth Dynasty who reigned for some forty-five years in the nineteenth century BC. This is the Middle Kingdom, about seven hundred years after the Great Pyramid.

He built two pyramids. The first, at Dahshur, is the one now called the Black Pyramid. It was built on poor ground and began to crack and settle during construction. So he started again at Hawara, at the mouth of the Fayoum, a region his dynasty had drained and farmed.

The second attempt was engineered with care. The burial chamber was cut from a single block of quartzite weighing around a hundred tonnes, lowered into a pit, and sealed with roofing slabs set in place by draining sand from beneath them. None of it stopped the robbers. Today the chamber and its passages are flooded with groundwater, and the interior cannot be entered.

Against the south face of this pyramid stood his mortuary temple. It was very large and unusually complicated. That temple is what later visitors called the Labyrinth. The site’s [guide to the Middle Kingdom pharaohs](https://travel2egypt.org/blog/middle-kingdom-pharaohs) sets him in context.

## What the ancient writers said

Four accounts matter. None was written within thirteen hundred years of the building’s construction.

**Herodotus**, in the fifth century BC, is the earliest and the most quoted. In Book 2 of the *Histories* he describes twelve roofed courts and three thousand rooms, half above ground and half below. He says he walked through the upper rooms himself. The lower ones he was not allowed to see, and was told they held the tombs of kings and of sacred crocodiles. His verdict was that the building surpassed the pyramids.

**Diodorus Siculus**, in the first century BC, is cooler. He attributes it to a king he calls Mendes, says a visitor could not find the way out without a guide, and suggests it inspired the labyrinth of Crete.

**Strabo** visited in the 20s BC and gives the most architectural account. He describes a palace made up of many palaces, as many as there were provinces of Egypt, with long covered passages and ceilings each made from a single slab of stone. He names the builder as Imandes, which is a recognisable echo of Amenemhat’s throne name.

**Pliny the Elder**, in the first century AD, places it in a list of labyrinths, gives it an age of 3,600 years, and notes that it had already been damaged by neighbouring townspeople.

Read them together and three cautions follow. The numbers are a guide’s numbers, repeated to a tourist. Herodotus did not count three thousand rooms. Second, he got the builders wrong by more than a thousand years, crediting twelve kings of a period close to his own. Third, the underground half of the building rests on one man being told about it. He says himself that he did not see it.

What the accounts do establish is simpler and still remarkable. For at least five hundred years a very large, many-roomed, stone-roofed building stood here, in good enough condition to impress well-travelled Greeks and Romans.

## Where the building went

It was quarried. From the Roman period onward the Labyrinth was treated as a supply of ready-cut stone. Fine limestone was carried off for building or burned for lime. Granite was broken up. What could not be used was left as chips.

Two canals finished the work. A medieval channel was cut through the site, and at the start of the twentieth century a larger irrigation canal was built along the same line. It raised the water table under the whole area, which is why the pyramid’s chambers are flooded and why digging here is difficult.

## What Petrie established

The site was identified in the 1840s. The Prussian scholar Karl Richard Lepsius dug south of the pyramid in 1843 and announced that he had found the Labyrinth. What he had mostly found were brick walls of later houses built on top of it.

Flinders Petrie arrived in 1888. He worked out that Lepsius’s walls were a Roman village and dug beneath them. There he found a vast bed of limestone chips, in places metres deep, lying on a level prepared foundation. He measured the area at roughly 300 by 240 metres, and reckoned that the temples of Karnak and Luxor would both fit inside it.

That was the Labyrinth: its floor, and the waste from its demolition. Of the walls, almost nothing. Petrie recovered fragments of columns, pieces of statues of gods including the crocodile god Sobek, and inscriptions naming Amenemhat III and his daughter Sobekneferu, who later ruled as king and seems to have completed the work.

He returned in 1911 and found more sculpture, including shrines with figures of the king. From the fragments and from Strabo he drew a reconstruction with rows of courts and shrines. It is a reasoned guess. It should be labelled as one wherever it is reproduced.

Petrie also tunnelled into the pyramid, reaching the flooded burial chamber in January 1889. It had been robbed in antiquity.

## Petrie’s methods, fairly judged

Petrie recorded more than anyone working in Egypt at the time. He measured, drew, and published quickly, and Hawara is far better documented because of him.

His standards were still those of the 1880s. Workmen were paid by the find. In the cemetery he kept the mummies with painted portraits and reburied or discarded most of the rest, which he counted in the thousands. Portraits were often cut away from their bodies. The finds were then divided between Cairo and his sponsors in England, and from there scattered across museums in several countries.

Much of what is unknown about Hawara today is unknown because of choices made in those seasons. Much of what is known is known for the same reason.

## What the scans can and cannot prove

In 2008 a team from Egypt’s National Research Institute of Astronomy and Geophysics and Ghent University, in a project funded by the Belgian artist Louis De Cordier and called the Mataha Expedition, surveyed the ground south of the pyramid. They reported a grid-like pattern of anomalies below the level of Petrie’s foundation, under the water table.

Related work was published in a peer-reviewed geophysics journal in 2010. A further study using electrical resistivity, published in 2024, also reported possible buried remains. Its authors stated that excavation would be needed to say what they are.

That sentence is the important one. Geophysics detects contrasts in the ground: wetter and drier, denser and looser, more and less conductive. It does not detect rooms. A regular pattern may be walls. It may also be foundation trenches, the edges of Petrie’s own excavations, or the behaviour of saturated ground. Waterlogged sites full of rubble are among the hardest to read.

More recently, images described as satellite scans of the Labyrinth have spread widely online, showing sharp-edged halls, shafts, and in some versions a large metallic object. These rest on proprietary processing that has not been independently tested. Treat them as claims.

The proposal behind the survey work is worth stating fairly. Its supporters argue that Petrie’s stone bed was not the Labyrinth’s floor but its roof, and that the building survives underneath. It is a testable idea. It has not yet been tested.

## The 2026 excavation

In May 2026 the Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities confirmed new fieldwork at Hawara, led by the Fayoum antiquities inspectorate. A team is now digging in the Labyrinth area south of the pyramid, and photographs from the summer show large dressed stone blocks exposed in a trench.

This is the step the surveys could not take. The question of what lies under Petrie’s stone bed is finally being put to the ground itself.

What the work has not yet produced, as of November 2026, is a published report. Large blocks in the Labyrinth area are what anyone would expect: Petrie found them too. Whether these belong to the known foundation or to something beneath it is what plans, levels and dates will settle.

Until then, a wall in a photograph is a wall in a photograph.

## Sorting the claims

| Kind of claim | Example | How much weight it bears |
|---|---|---|
| Ancient description | Herodotus’s three thousand rooms | Shows a huge building stood here. The figures are hearsay. |
| Excavated evidence | Petrie’s foundation bed, columns and inscriptions | Firm. Fixes the site, the scale and the builder. |
| Scholarly reconstruction | Petrie’s plan of courts and shrines | An informed guess built from fragments and Strabo. |
| Published geophysics | The 2010 and 2024 survey papers | Real anomalies. What they are is unknown without digging. |
| Unpublished scans | Satellite images of halls and shafts | Unverified. |
| Current excavation | Blocks exposed in the 2026 trench | Promising. Awaiting publication. |

## The other Hawara

A visitor who comes for the Labyrinth should not leave without looking north.

In the Roman period, some eighteen centuries after Amenemhat, the ground beside his pyramid became a cemetery for the prosperous families of the Fayoum. They mummified their dead in the Egyptian way and placed over the face a portrait painted on a wooden panel, in a style that was entirely Greek and Roman.

Petrie found eighty-one of these portrait mummies in his first season and about seventy more in 1911. Under the head of one woman he also found a papyrus roll of Homer’s *Iliad*.

Those paintings are now in London, Cairo, Manchester, New York and elsewhere. They are the subject of a separate piece, [The Fayoum Portraits](https://travel2egypt.org/blog/fayoum-portraits). Keep the two Hawaras apart in your mind. The pyramid and the Labyrinth are Middle Kingdom. The portraits are Roman. The same sand, and the better part of two thousand years between them.

## How to visit

Hawara lies about nine kilometres south-east of Fayoum city. From Cairo or Giza it is a drive of around two hours, depending heavily on traffic leaving the city.

It is usually visited as part of a day in the Fayoum rather than alone. The natural partners are the other Middle Kingdom and early pyramids nearby: Lahun, the pyramid of Senusret II, a few kilometres away, and Meidum, the collapsed pyramid on the road back toward Cairo. Together they make a day about how pyramid building rose, failed and was rethought.

There is little shade and no café. The ground is rough underfoot. In the cooler months the site is comfortable all day. From May to September, go early.

A guide matters more here than almost anywhere in Egypt. At Karnak the building explains itself. At Hawara there is nothing to explain itself with. For travellers with a further day, there is a stop of a very different age. South of the Fayoum, in the desert at Mount Qalamoun, stands the Monastery of Saint Samuel the Confessor. Samuel was a Coptic monk born in 597, and he settled here in the seventh century. It is a working monastery, not a ruin, and the cave where he lived is the heart of a visit. ## What to confirm before going

- **Opening and tickets.** Hawara appears on the Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities ticket list as an open site. Confirm current hours and price before travelling.
- **The pyramid interior.** Closed. The chambers are flooded.
- **Excavation areas.** Fieldwork is in progress, and parts of the Labyrinth area may be fenced. Do not expect to approach a working trench.
- **Photography.** Phone photography is normally free at open sites. Rules near active excavations can be stricter.
- **Facilities.** Assume none. Bring water.

## Who it suits, and who it doesn’t

It suits readers. If you have read Herodotus, or simply like the idea of standing where a famous lost building stood and working out what can be known about it, Hawara is one of the most rewarding stops in Egypt.

It suits people on a second trip, who have seen Giza and Luxor and want to understand the centuries in between.

It does not suit anyone who needs a monument to look like a monument. On a first, short visit to Egypt, a day here costs you a day somewhere more generous. And it will disappoint anyone who has come for the underground city in the videos. That city is, for now, a hypothesis.

## In sum

Hawara asks more of a visitor than most Egyptian sites and gives less to the camera. What it offers is a rare kind of honesty. The greatest building in Egypt, by the judgement of the people who saw it, is a field of chips, and you can stand in it and think about how anything is known about the past at all.

The Labyrinth existed. It was vast. It was taken apart. Whether part of it survives beneath the water table is a real question, and the answer will come from a trench, not a scan.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you visit the Egyptian Labyrinth?

You can visit the site at Hawara where it stood. You will see the pyramid of Amenemhat III and the Labyrinth’s foundation area, with stone fragments and broken columns. There are no standing rooms or corridors to enter.

### Has the Labyrinth at Hawara been found?

Its location and foundations were identified by Flinders Petrie in 1888. Geophysical surveys since 2008 have detected anomalies beneath the foundation level that may be buried structures. An excavation began in 2026 to test this. Its results have not yet been published.

### What did Herodotus say about the Labyrinth?

In Book 2 of the *Histories* he described twelve roofed courts and three thousand rooms, half of them underground, and judged it greater than the pyramids. He saw the upper rooms himself and was only told about the lower ones.

### Who built the Labyrinth?

Amenemhat III, of the Twelfth Dynasty, in the nineteenth century BC. It was the mortuary temple attached to his pyramid at Hawara. His daughter Sobekneferu appears to have completed it.

### Can you go inside the Hawara pyramid?

No. The entrance passage and burial chamber are flooded by groundwater and closed to visitors.

### How far is Hawara from Cairo?

About two hours by road, depending on traffic. It is around nine kilometres from Fayoum city and is normally combined with the pyramids of Lahun and Meidum in a single day.
