## Opening

The temples south of the Aswan High Dam aren’t where they were built. When Lake Nasser rose in the 1960s, many Nubian monuments along the old riverbank were about to go under the water, and several were saved: cut into blocks and rebuilt on higher ground. Three of them now stand together on New Kalabsha, an island just upstream of the dam: the Temple of Kalabsha, the rock-cut Temple of Beit el-Wali and the small Kiosk of Kertassi. This shared day takes a small group of four to eight across to them by motor boat.

---

## Why the day runs this way

The day is long for the distances, not for the temples: a pickup that gathers the group across Aswan’s hotels and ships, the drive south past the High Dam, the boat out to the island and back, lunch at a local restaurant, and the drive home. The morning start matters because the island has no shade. On the island, Kalabsha comes first because it is the largest of the three and takes the longest visit; Beit el-Wali and Kertassi follow, a few minutes’ walk apart.

## Your day

### Morning · Across to New Kalabsha

**About 08:00 · Pickup from your hotel or ship**
Your driver and Egyptologist collect you, and the air-conditioned minivan gathers the rest of the group before heading south through the town.

**The boat to the island**
A short drive past the High Dam to the landing on the lake shore, where the motor boat waits. The crossing to New Kalabsha takes about fifteen minutes, with the dam wall to one side.

**The Temple of Kalabsha**
Built under the Roman emperor Augustus, around 30 BC, over an older Ptolemaic temple, for Mandulis, a Nubian god linked with Horus. The visit runs from the causeway through the pylon, the court and the hypostyle hall to the sanctuary. It was rebuilt here, near the dam, when the lake rose over its original site.
*Sites:* Temple of Kalabsha

**The Temple of Beit el-Wali**
A few minutes’ walk away, and older by more than a thousand years: a small temple cut into the rock for Ramses II early in the 13th century BC. Its reliefs of the king’s campaigns still keep some of their colour. The inner rooms are dim, so bring a small torch or use your phone’s light.
*Sites:* Temple of Beit el-Wali

**The Kiosk of Kertassi**
A small Roman-period kiosk of columns at the water’s edge, and the last stop on the island. Allow about two and a half hours on the island in all.
*Sites:* Kiosk of Kertassi
