## Opening

Within a few kilometres on Luxor’s West Bank lie the tombs of pharaohs and queens, two of Egypt’s best-preserved mortuary temples and the village of the craftsmen who made all of it. Most visitors cross the river for a few hours. This private day stays on the West Bank and takes six sites seriously, from the royal valleys to the craftsmen’s village, until it reads as what it was: a planned royal city of the dead.

---

## Why the day runs this way

The 07:00 start isn’t politeness; it’s how you reach the tombs before the heat and the crowds do. The Valley of the Kings comes first, then the queens, then the temples, and the day ends at Deir el-Medina, which answers the question the royal tombs leave hanging: who actually made all this? With your own Egyptologist, you move at a considered pace, with context rather than commentary.

## Your day

### Morning · The royal valleys

**07:00 · Pickup from your hotel**
Your driver and Egyptologist collect you in a private air-conditioned car.

**The Valley of the Kings**
The burial ground of the New Kingdom pharaohs, about 1550–1070 BC: more than sixty tombs under a natural pyramid-shaped peak. Your ticket covers three tombs, chosen with your guide from those open on the day. Tutankhamun’s tomb is an extra ticket, bought at the site: it’s small and his treasure has left, but its painted burial chamber is among the most intimate in the valley.
*Sites:* Valley of the Kings

**The Valley of the Queens**
Two kilometres south. Your standard ticket covers the tombs open on the day. The tomb of Nefertari, Ramses II’s principal wife, buried around 1255 BC, is closed to visitors at present; if it reopens, its ticket is an extra, bought at the site. Its painted walls have kept their colour with extraordinary fidelity, so visits are timed and numbers limited, to protect the paint.
*Sites:* Valley of the Queens

**The Colossi of Memnon**
Two seated quartzite figures of Amenhotep III, eighteen metres tall, all that stands of his great mortuary temple. The Greeks heard them "sing" at dawn, until a Roman repair silenced the cracked statue.
*Sites:* Colossi of Memnon

**Optional extras, if you’ve chosen them**
- The tomb of Tutankhamun (KV62): extra ticket, bought at the site
- The tomb of Nefertari: closed at present; if it reopens, an extra ticket, bought at the site

### Midday · Hatshepsut and lunch

**The Temple of Hatshepsut**
Built for the woman who ruled as pharaoh in her own right, around 1473–1458 BC, by her architect Senenmut: three colonnaded terraces cut into the cliff, with reliefs of the voyage to Punt. Some of her images were defaced after her death, most likely under Thutmose III.
*Sites:* Temple of Hatshepsut

**Lunch at a local restaurant (included)**
Drinks aren’t included.

### Afternoon · Medinet Habu and Deir el-Medina

**Medinet Habu**
Ramses III’s mortuary temple, around 1180 BC, and the most underrated site on the West Bank: exceptionally well preserved, with outer walls recording his wars against the Sea Peoples, one of the few contemporary accounts of them.
*Sites:* Medinet Habu

**Deir el-Medina**
The walled village of the royal tomb builders: houses, wells, a temple and their own small, steep tombs, painted for themselves by the same craftsmen who decorated the royal tombs. Their papers record strikes, petitions, debts and love poems.
*Sites:* Deir el-Medina
