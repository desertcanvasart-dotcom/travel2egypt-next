There is a moment in the making of sayadeya when a careful cook does something that looks like a mistake. The onions, sliced and frying, are taken past golden, past brown, to the very edge of burning — and it is exactly there, in that dark, bitter-sweet concentration, that the dish is decided. The onions and their liquid go into the rice, the rice takes on the colour of wet sand at dusk, and the day's fish is laid on top. That is sayadeya: the fisherman's dish, from *sayyad*, fisherman, and it belongs to every stretch of coast where Egyptian boats come in.

Which is precisely what makes it worth following. Ask for sayadeya in Alexandria, in Port Said, in Suez, and you will be served recognisably the same idea — fish, rice, deeply browned onion, cumin, lemon — and yet each city will tell you, with complete confidence, that theirs is the true one. The recipes vary less than the pride does. But the pride is the interesting part, because these are three different kinds of port, made by three different histories, and a dish this simple has room for all of them.

### The dish itself

Sayadeya is a technique before it is a recipe. The foundation is the onion, cooked far darker than almost anywhere else in Egyptian cooking, then married to rice and the cooking liquid — often a quick stock from the fish trimmings — so that the grain absorbs colour, sweetness, and a faint smokiness. Cumin is the constant spice; black pepper is common; some kitchens add tomato, some consider tomato a scandal; chile appears where the house likes heat. The fish is whatever the boats brought: mullet, sea bass, bluefish, sometimes shrimp folded through the rice instead. It is fried or baked, whole or in fillets, and served over or beside the rice with lemon and, usually, a sharp little salad and tahini.

How old is it? Here honesty matters more than romance. Egyptians have eaten fish with grain since there were Egyptians — the fishing scenes are on tomb walls, and river and sea fish alike are documented in the ancient record. But that is the food system, not the dish. Sayadeya as a named preparation belongs to the modern coastal kitchen, and no serious account traces this exact plate to antiquity. Call the deep history a plausible lineage and the dish itself a coastal tradition of the last few centuries, and you are on solid ground.

### Alexandria: the documented heart

If sayadeya has a capital, it is Alexandria — the city where the ritual around the dish is as codified as the dish itself. An Alexandrian fish meal begins before the cooking does: you go to the ice, you choose your fish by eye and by weight, and only then do you decide its fate — grilled, fried, *singari* (butterflied and baked with vegetables and aromatics, sayadeya's flashier cousin), or over sayadeya rice. The choosing is not a tourist performance; it is how the city eats, and surrendering that choice to a menu is regarded, gently, as not quite knowing how things are done.

Alexandria's version of the dish reflects Alexandria's palate, which runs brighter and hotter than Cairo's — this is the city of aggressively garlicky kebda Eskandarani and of ful sharpened with cumin and chile. Its sayadeya rice tends toward generosity with the browned onion, and its tables assume the Mediterranean's repertoire: shrimp, squid, crab, and roe alongside the day's fish. A century of Greek and Italian kitchens left their traces in the city's bread and pastry rather than in this dish, but they left something subtler too — a port's habit of treating outside influence as raw material rather than threat. Alexandrian sayadeya is Egyptian food with a Mediterranean posture.

### The canal cities: younger water, sharper claims

Port Said did not exist until 1859. It was called into being by the Suez Canal, as Ismailia was, and Suez — ancient as an anchorage — was remade by the same project. These are cities built by labour migration, by traffic between two seas, by generations of workers, sailors, and traders passing through. Their food culture carries that history: fish restaurants clustered near the water, a seriousness about the catch that rivals Alexandria's, and an openness to whatever arrived on the last ship.

And here the honest traveller should know what is documented and what is local conviction. That the canal cities cook superb fish is beyond argument — Port Said's reputation for seafood is national, and Egyptians drive from Cairo for it. That their sayadeya is *distinct* — darker, some will tell you, or spicier, or more austere, closer to what a fisherman would actually cook aboard — is what you will hear at the table, said with the certainty of scripture. The paperwork behind those claims is thinner than the pride. Regional foodways in Egypt are centres of association, not exclusive ownership, and dishes travel with marriage and work faster than any recipe can be fenced. What is certainly true is that a plate of sayadeya in Port Said arrives in a different city: younger, saltier in its history, facing two seas at once. Whether the rice is truly darker there is a question best settled in person, which is rather the point.

### What the differences teach

A dish with three passports is a lesson in how Egyptian food actually works. Sayadeya is a system — onion taken dark, rice made to carry it, the day's catch on top — and every kitchen along the northern water tunes that system to its own sea, its own market, and its own idea of itself. Alexandria tunes it cosmopolitan; the canal cities tune it maritime and proud; the family kitchens of the Delta, where rice country meets the coast, tune it homeward. No tuning is the original, because the original is the system, not the setting.

This is also why the arguments never end, and why they are worth listening to rather than adjudicating. When a man in Port Said tells you Alexandrians make sayadeya for tourists, and a woman in Alexandria tells you the canal cities burn their onions out of carelessness rather than conviction, you are not hearing recipe criticism. You are hearing three coastal identities using one plate to explain themselves.

### Eating it well

Sayadeya is a lunch dish at heart, eaten when the catch is freshest, and the best of it is found where fish is chosen rather than ordered — the fish markets and the streets of fish restaurants near the water in any of the three cities, where the ice is out front and the scale is part of the conversation. Choose your fish first; ask for sayadeya rice with it; accept the salad and the tahini; add lemon with confidence and chile to taste. Egyptian food is rarely hot by default, and this dish is no exception — the heat, where it exists, is yours to control.

Go with time to spare. A proper fish lunch on the Egyptian coast is not fast food that happens to contain fish; it is an unhurried institution with tea at the end of it, and the pace is part of the flavour.

### FAQ

**What exactly is sayadeya?**

Fish served over rice that has been cooked with deeply browned onions and their liquid, seasoned principally with cumin — a dish of Egypt's Mediterranean and canal coasts. The name comes from *sayyad*, fisherman. The fish varies with the day's catch; the dark, onion-stained rice is the constant.

**Is sayadeya spicy?**

Not by default. It is savoury, faintly sweet from the onions, and sharpened with lemon. Chile appears in some kitchens, particularly in Alexandria and the canal cities, but heat is usually added at the table rather than built in.

**What is the difference between sayadeya and singari?**

They are cousins from the same coast. Sayadeya is defined by its rice; the fish completes it. Singari is defined by the fish — butterflied, dressed with vegetables and aromatics, and baked or grilled — and any rice is a companion rather than the point. Many Alexandrian fish restaurants offer both, and choosing between them is part of the ritual.

**Where is the best place to try it?**

Anywhere the fish is chosen from ice rather than a menu. Alexandria's fish-market district and its seafood streets are the classic setting; Port Said's waterfront restaurants are a pilgrimage Egyptians make from Cairo; Suez is quieter and closer to how the dish began. Each city will tell you the other two do it wrong. Order it in at least two of them and join the argument.
