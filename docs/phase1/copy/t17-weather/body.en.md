## Egypt's Weather

"Egypt's weather" is one of those phrases that flattens a substantial geographic reality. The country runs more than 1,000 kilometres from the Mediterranean coast in the north to the Sudanese border in the south, with a desert mass several times larger than France stretching east and west of the inhabited Nile Valley. The Mediterranean coast has mild rainy winters and humid summers. Cairo has hot dry summers and surprisingly cool winter nights. Luxor and Aswan have temperatures that can approach 50°C in midsummer and feel warm-pleasant in winter. The Red Sea coast is warm almost year-round. The Sinai mountains can see frost overnight in winter. The Western Desert oases swing between hot days and cold nights in the same 24 hours. Treating Egypt's weather as a single thing is the single most common mistake travellers make when choosing a date.

This is a working guide to the country's actual climate zones, the seasonal windows that matter for travellers, and the practical implications for what to pack, when to visit, and what to expect.

## The Country's Five Climates

For practical traveller purposes, Egypt divides into five distinct climate zones, each with its own annual rhythm.

The Mediterranean coast — Alexandria, Marsa Matruh, and the strip from Sallum near the Libyan border to El Arish near Gaza — has the country's only meaningfully Mediterranean climate. Summers are hot but moderated by sea breezes, with temperatures typically 28-32°C in the day and 22-25°C at night. Winters are mild and the rainiest part of Egypt, with November-through-March bringing 20-30 days of light to moderate rain across the season, daytime temperatures around 15-18°C, and night temperatures 8-12°C. Storms can roll in from the Mediterranean and produce dramatic but short-lived rain; flooding occasionally affects Alexandria's older streets.

Cairo and the Nile Delta sit in a transitional zone — too far from the coast to share the Mediterranean's full influence, too far from the desert to be fully arid. Summers are hot and dry, with daytime highs averaging 35°C and peaks reaching 40°C and above. Nights are cooler — typically 22-25°C — providing genuine relief. Winters are mild during the day (18-20°C high) but cool at night (8-12°C low), with December and January bringing the year's coldest nights. Annual rainfall in Cairo is minimal — usually around 25-30mm a year — and concentrated in winter.

Upper Egypt — Luxor, Aswan, and the Nile Valley south of Asyut — has the most extreme climate. Summer daytime highs regularly exceed 40°C, with peaks reaching 45°C and occasionally 50°C in July and August. The dry desert air makes the heat technically more bearable than equivalent humid temperatures elsewhere, but the cumulative effect on a full sightseeing day is genuinely difficult. Winters are warm-pleasant — typically 22-28°C in the day, 8-14°C at night — and the peak tourist season runs December through February. Spring and autumn (March, April, October, November) are warmer than the European spring and autumn but more comfortable than midsummer.

The Red Sea coast — Hurghada, Sharm El Sheikh, Marsa Alam, Dahab, Taba — runs warmer than Cairo year-round and is the country's most reliable year-round destination. Summer daytime temperatures average 32-37°C; winter daytime temperatures average 22-26°C. Sea temperatures are warm throughout the year, ranging from approximately 22°C in February (the coldest month) to 28-29°C in August. Diving and snorkelling operate year-round; the cooler months require a thicker wetsuit. Rainfall is minimal — fewer than 10 days a year across the Red Sea cities.

The deserts and Sinai mountains have their own pattern. The Western Desert oases (Bahariya, Farafra, Dakhla, Kharga, Siwa) have extreme diurnal swings — summer days exceeding 40°C and nights cooling to 25°C, winter days at 22-25°C and nights dropping to 8-12°C. The Sinai interior — particularly the high country around Saint Catherine's at roughly 1,600 metres above sea level — has cool nights year-round and genuinely cold winter nights, with frost frequently in December-February and occasional snow at the higher elevations. The mountain trek to the summit of Mount Sinai for sunrise involves walking in temperatures that can drop near freezing in winter.

## The Seasons

The Egyptian travel year divides into four distinct seasons that affect different destinations differently.

Autumn — October and November — is one of the year's best travel windows. The summer heat retreats; nights cool to comfortable levels; tourist numbers have not yet built up to peak season. Daytime temperatures in Cairo run 28-32°C; in Luxor and Aswan, 30-35°C; on the Red Sea, 28-32°C with warm sea temperatures. November is the more reliably comfortable of the two months.

Winter — December, January, February — is peak Egyptian tourist season for inland sightseeing. Daytime temperatures are pleasant across the country (Cairo 18-20°C, Luxor 22-25°C, Red Sea 22-25°C), and the famous sites are at their most comfortable for walking. The trade-off is crowds — December and January in particular fill Luxor and Aswan hotels and Nile cruises, with the Christmas and New Year week the busiest of the year. Nights are cool to cold: Cairo and Cairo Delta nights regularly drop to 8-12°C; Luxor and Aswan nights drop to similar lows; Saint Catherine's and the Sinai interior can drop below freezing. Pack layers, particularly for early-morning visits to sites and for late evenings.

Spring — March, April, May — is more variable than the other seasons. Temperatures climb steadily; the Khamsin sand-storm season runs through the months (see the next section); but March and April remain workable for inland travel before the heat reaches its summer peak. By May, daytime temperatures in Luxor and Aswan have reached 35-40°C and the inland season is winding down. April is the most popular spring month, helped by the timing of Easter (Western, Coptic, or both) and the Sham El Nessim public holiday that follows Coptic Easter.

Summer — June, July, August, September — is the season most foreign travellers should avoid for inland Egypt. Daytime temperatures in Cairo regularly exceed 35°C and frequently exceed 40°C. Luxor and Aswan reach 42-48°C during the day, with stone surfaces at archaeological sites radiating heat that can make midday visits physically dangerous. The Red Sea coast remains workable — daytime temperatures of 32-37°C with sea breezes — and the Mediterranean coast (Alexandria and the western strip) is at its busiest as Egyptians escape the inland heat. Summer is the best season for Mediterranean coast and Red Sea travel; it is the worst for everything else. Travellers who must visit inland in summer should plan around early-morning starts (gates at major sites open at 6:00 or 7:00 am), midday breaks in air-conditioning, and late-afternoon and evening site visits where opening hours allow.

## The Khamsin

The Khamsin — from the Arabic word for "fifty," referring to the approximate 50-day window during which the phenomenon occurs — is the spring sand-and-dust-storm pattern that most foreign travellers have not encountered before and that catches many off guard.

The Khamsin is a hot, dry, dust-laden wind that blows in from the Sahara during the spring months, typically late March through early May. The winds form when low-pressure systems pass across North Africa, drawing hot air northwards from the deep desert. The wind can raise temperatures by 10-15°C within hours, reduce visibility substantially, fill the air with fine reddish dust that coats every surface, and irritate eyes, throat, and respiratory systems. Severe Khamsins can shut down airports, halt outdoor activities at archaeological sites, and produce two to three days of unpleasant conditions before the system passes.

Not every spring brings severe Khamsin storms, and the season can have many calm days. But travellers planning trips in March, April, or May should know that the weather can shift dramatically and that any single date is statistically more variable than a date in winter or autumn. The standard practical responses: carry a scarf or buff for face covering during dusty conditions; bring eye drops or rinse if conditions affect contact lens wearers; expect that a planned outdoor activity may need rescheduling.

Cairo, the Nile Delta, and Upper Egypt are most exposed to Khamsin events. The Red Sea coast feels the heat-and-humidity change but receives less of the dust. The Mediterranean coast is generally cooler and less dusty during Khamsin events.

## When to Visit

Different traveller priorities point to different optimal windows.

For inland sightseeing — Cairo, Luxor, Aswan, the West Bank temples — the optimal window is late October through early March. The November-through-February core is the most comfortable; October and early March bring slightly higher temperatures but smaller crowds. December and January carry the highest prices and tightest bookings, particularly the Christmas-New Year fortnight. Late March through April is workable but Khamsin-prone. May is too hot for most travellers.

For the Red Sea coast — Hurghada, Sharm El Sheikh, Marsa Alam, Dahab — the destination works year-round. December to March is comfortable for swimming and snorkelling; April to October is hot but tolerable, with the August peak being the hardest. The diving community has its own preferences: spring and autumn for the best visibility and most active marine life; summer for the warmest water; winter for the lowest prices and smallest crowds.

For Alexandria and the Mediterranean coast — the high summer months (June, July, August) are the local season, with Egyptian families filling Alexandria's Corniche and the western coastal resorts. October through May is quieter and milder, with November-March bringing the Mediterranean winter rains and cooler conditions.

For family travel — the tips-for-families guide covers the broader operational logic. Briefly: October through April is the family window, December and January are peak family season, and summer is genuinely difficult with young children at inland sites.

For desert travel — Bahariya, the White Desert, Siwa Oasis, the Western Desert circuits — October through March is the safe window. April and May can work but Khamsin risk is real. June through September is genuinely demanding even for experienced desert travellers.

For Sinai trekking — Mount Sinai, Saint Catherine's, the broader Sinai interior — October through April is the practical window. Winter nights are cold (frost is common at altitude); summer days are hot. The dawn climb to the summit of Mount Sinai is workable year-round but more comfortable in cooler months.

For Nile cruises — October through April is the standard cruise season. Summer cruises are operated by some lines but with substantially reduced demand; the ships' onboard temperatures are managed, but the shore excursions to temples in 40°C heat are genuinely demanding.

## Clothing and Climate Implications

The packing list shifts substantially by season.

Summer is about the sun as much as the heat. Start sightseeing early, before stone and sand have warmed up; wear light, loose, breathable clothing, a wide-brimmed hat, and sunglasses; and reapply sunscreen through the day.

Winter means packing for two temperatures. Days are warm, but nights turn cool, and in the desert genuinely cold, so a light jacket or sweater is necessary rather than optional; early-morning site visits and evenings on deck are when it earns its place. For Saint Catherine's, the dawn climb of Mount Sinai, or a night in the White Desert, bring properly warm layers, since temperatures there can drop near or below freezing.

Spring adds the Khamsin: a scarf or buff for dusty days, and eye drops for contact-lens wearers, as covered above.

Whatever the season, drink water steadily through the day, especially when touring outdoor sites. The dry air lets dehydration build before thirst makes it obvious.
