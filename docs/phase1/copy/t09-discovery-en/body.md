In November 1922, a step was found in the [Valley of the Kings](/guide/luxor/valley-of-the-kings).

Workers on Howard Carter's excavation found it in a corner of the valley that many believed exhausted. The step was the top of a stairway, and the stairway led down to a sealed tomb.

Weeks later, Carter and his patron Lord Carnarvon looked through a small hole in the sealed doorway. Candlelight fell on gilded couches, boxes, chariots, guardian figures and vessels. Carnarvon asked whether he could see anything, and Carter is said to have answered, "Yes, wonderful things."

That moment made Tutankhamun the most famous pharaoh in the world.

## Why this discovery was special

Tutankhamun wasn't a great king in life. A young 18th Dynasty king, he came to the throne after Akhenaten's religious reform and was involved in restoring the traditional religious order centred on Amun. His reign was short, and he died in his late teens.

His fame doesn't come from anything he achieved in life. It comes from his tomb surviving nearly intact.

Many royal tombs in the Valley of the Kings were looted in antiquity. The larger and better-known a tomb was, the earlier it was found and stripped. Tutankhamun's tomb was small, and its entrance was hidden by later work and debris, so it was never emptied.

The tomb shows traces of ancient intrusion. Even so, most of its contents remained. A royal tomb with this much of its burial equipment still in place had never been found in the modern era of archaeology.

## Howard Carter's search

Howard Carter first came to Egypt as a painter. He began by copying wall paintings, and through long years on the Theban west bank he became an excavator who knew the Valley of the Kings exceptionally well.

He believed Tutankhamun's tomb had not yet been found. Small objects bearing the king's name and fragments linked to his burial supported that belief. Earlier excavators thought the valley had nothing major left to give; Carter didn't accept it.

With Lord Carnarvon's funding, he searched for years. With no results, there was talk of stopping. In what was treated as a final chance, re-examining part of the valley, his workers found the step in November 1922. Below it, the stairway led down to a sealed entrance, with another blocked entrance further in.

The discovery was dramatic; the work that followed was longer and more mundane. Opening a tomb isn't the end. Recording, photographing, conserving and removing the contents took roughly ten years.

## What lay inside, in brief

Over five thousand items were packed into the tomb's four small rooms, from gilded furniture and chariots to the golden mask. Where each object lay mattered as much as the object itself: knowing what was in which room, and how the rooms were used, is what made the tomb so valuable for understanding royal burial. We go through the tomb room by room, and the golden mask, in [Tutankhamun's tomb (KV62): what was inside and how to visit](/blog/the-story-of-king-tutankhamun-tomb).

## Records that changed archaeology

What matters about Carter's excavation isn't only the richness of the find. Before removing anything, his team photographed it, numbered it, recorded its position and noted its condition. That is standard practice now; for the time, it was very thorough.

Thanks to that record, KV62 is not simply "a tomb where treasure came out" but a tomb whose burial can be reconstructed. The objects survived, and so did the relationship between the objects and the space.

The tomb showed the public, in a form anyone could follow, archaeology moving from treasure hunting to a discipline built on recording.

## Egypt and the question of ownership

1922, the year the tomb was found, mattered for Egyptian politics too. Egypt was moving from British influence towards formal independence, and who owned antiquities was not only an academic question. The era when foreign excavators took their finds abroad was changing.

Tutankhamun's grave goods stayed in Egypt. It was a clear expression of the idea that Egypt's cultural heritage belongs to Egypt.

## The curse, and what it isn't

The story of "the pharaoh's curse" is bound up with this tomb. One trigger was Lord Carnarvon's death soon after the opening; newspapers made it a sensation, and the idea spread that a curse fell on those who disturbed the tomb.

No famous curse text was written in the tomb. Carnarvon's death is thought to have been caused by an infection, at a time before antibiotics were widely available, when such infections could be fatal.

The curse story is less archaeology than a product of the press and popular culture. Old tombs do carry real health risks (dust, mould, poor ventilation), but they are physical, not supernatural. We look at the legend in more detail in [the curse of King Tut's tomb](/blog/the-curse-of-king-tuts-tomb).

The curse isn't central to understanding the discovery. What is central is how the tomb survived, how it was recorded and how the world received it.

## Worldwide Egypt fever

The discovery set off a worldwide wave of Egyptomania. Fashion, jewellery, architecture, film and advertising in the 1920s took up Egyptian-style design; the golden mask, cobras, wings, geometric patterns and pharaonic imagery merged with the Art Deco aesthetic.

The press made it possible. The find was covered at length in the newspapers and reached a world audience through photographs and stories. For the first time, an archaeological excavation became a mass-media event.

Tutankhamun's fame wasn't made by ancient greatness alone. The timing of the discovery, the way it was reported, the visual power of the gold and the story of an "almost intact royal tomb" all layered on top of each other.

## The tomb and its treasures today

The tomb, KV62, is in the Valley of the Kings on [Luxor](/guide/luxor)'s west bank, and the main collection of its grave goods is now at [the Grand Egyptian Museum](/blog/the-grand-egyptian-museum) in [Giza](/guide/giza). For what is where, see [where Tutankhamun is now](/blog/where-is-tutankhamun-now); for the tomb itself and what to expect on a visit, see [our guide to Tutankhamun's tomb](/blog/the-story-of-king-tutankhamun-tomb).

## Why it still matters

Tutankhamun wasn't Egypt's most powerful king. His tomb showed what an ancient Egyptian royal burial was, in a form no one had seen before: beyond the beauty of the gold, furniture, clothing, ritual objects, everyday things and the preparations for the afterlife all remained together in one tomb.

The discovery shaped archaeology, museums, the debate over cultural ownership, popular culture and tourism. Tutankhamun became a figure of history less through what he did in life than through the moment he was found.

It was also chance: a small tomb, a hidden entrance, an excavator who didn't give up and twentieth-century media that carried the story round the world.

Seeing Tutankhamun's tomb, or its treasures, isn't only seeing ancient Egypt. It is also seeing how the modern world discovered, recorded, owned and imagined it.
