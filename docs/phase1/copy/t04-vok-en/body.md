In the [Valley of the Kings](/guide/luxor/valley-of-the-kings), don't try to see everything.

More than sixty tombs are known, and including unfinished pits and small chambers, more traces exist underground. Tombs a traveller can enter on one visit are limited. Tombs open also rotate for conservation. Some tombs need supplementary tickets.

What matters isn't the count.

The Valley of the Kings isn't a place for collecting tombs — it is a place for reading how New Kingdom kings thought about the afterworld. Corridors, ceilings, burial chambers, the solar boat, gods of the underworld, the night sky, serpents, gates. Wall paintings are not ornament — they were the wayfinding by which the king passed the world of night and was reborn with the morning sun.

A good visit can stand on three or four tombs. Combining tombs of different periods, different decoration, and different scale.

## First, things to know

The Valley of the Kings is in a dry limestone valley on [Luxor](/guide/luxor)'s west bank.

From the 18th to the 20th Dynasty, New Kingdom kings cut rock-cut tombs here. Tombs hidden in the rock, not visible from afar like pyramids. To deter ancient looting, and with the symbolic meaning of being buried beneath al-Qurn, the natural pyramid-shaped peak overlooking the valley.

Hidden didn't mean safe. Most tombs were looted in antiquity. Even Tutankhamun's tomb, famous as the exception, wasn't completely untouched.

Even so, the Valley of the Kings' value isn't only treasure. More so, it is in the surviving wall paintings and spaces. What afterworld kings imagined and what words and images they used to pass death. A place where this is still readable.

This post is about choosing tombs. For the practical layout of the site first, read [a simple guide to the Valley of the Kings](/blog/a-simple-guide-to-the-valley-of-the-kings).

## KV17 Seti I

If you choose one special tomb, Seti I's can be the first candidate.

KV17 is one of the most beautiful, most finished tombs in the valley. Deep corridors, fine decoration, very high painting and carving quality. Not painted on the wall alone — much is relief plus colour layered, with weight in the space itself.

A supplementary-ticket tomb with high entry fee. For those who want to see the peak of Valley of the Kings art, it is worth it.

Don't rush here. Beyond colour intensity, look at line precision, figures of gods, ceilings, corridor depth. You see clearly that the royal tomb wasn't merely an underground room but architecture building the journey after death.

## KV9 Ramesses V / Ramesses VI

For first-time Valley of the Kings visitors, KV9 is a very strong choice.

This tomb was begun for Ramesses V and expanded by Ramesses VI. Long corridors, rich colour, legible walls, and the astronomical ceiling of the burial chamber are striking. The sky goddess Nut is drawn large, with the sun's death and rebirth visually conveyed.

Often less expensive than Seti I's, and visually more satisfying than Tutankhamun's for many.

Many ancient visitor graffiti remain. Traces in Greek and Latin show that the Valley of the Kings was visited not only today but from antiquity.

## KV62 Tutankhamun

Set expectations right for Tutankhamun's tomb.

The most famous Egyptian tomb in the world but not the most magnificent in the Valley of the Kings. Small tomb, decoration limited to the burial chamber. The golden mask and most treasures aren't inside the tomb. They are in museums now.

KV62 has its own meaning still.

The artefacts Howard Carter discovered here in 1922 changed the world's image of ancient Egypt. Standing in the small underground space, you can't help thinking about why this tomb alone had this special fate.

Visit Tutankhamun's tomb not for visual lavishness but for the archaeological story and standing where the young king actually lay. That story is told in full in [the story of King Tutankhamun's tomb](/blog/the-story-of-king-tutankhamun-tomb).

## KV11 Ramesses III

Ramesses III's tomb has a good balance of scale and legibility.

Long corridors, rich decoration, often included in the standard ticket range, with high satisfaction among them. Ramesses III was an important late New Kingdom king; the tomb carries beyond royal afterworld scenes — also scenes related to offerings and goods, with a slightly different life feel than other tombs.

For those who don't want to add many supplementary tickets, KV11 is a good axis. Large, colour-retained, with the proper underground feel of the valley.

## KV2 Ramesses IV

Ramesses IV's tomb is easy to enter even for first-timers.

The corridor is relatively wide, the structure is legible, the burial chamber holds a large sarcophagus. Relatively easy to see even for people who don't do well with narrow passages or sharp climbs.

In the Valley of the Kings, the most spectacular tombs aren't always the most comfortable. KV2 balances clarity and impact. Not bad as the first tomb of a valley visit.

## KV57 Horemheb

Horemheb's tomb suits people who want to see both the finished work and the process.

Horemheb was a late 18th Dynasty king who took power after the chaos of the Amarna era. In his tomb, finished decoration and unfinished sketches appear side by side. Red and black lines, correction marks, the stage before carving. Very interesting for understanding how tombs were made.

For people who want only perfectly finished walls, it can feel quiet. For people who want to read the craftsmen's work, it is one of the most valuable tombs in the valley.

## KV34 Thutmose III

Thutmose III's tomb takes a little stamina.

The entrance is up high; you climb steps to enter. Interior decoration differs from the dense, colourful Ramesside tombs. On a white-ish background, the line drawings are simple and old-style.

So at first it can look quiet. That's what's interesting. The Valley of the Kings' decoration wasn't always like KV9 or KV17. See Thutmose III's tomb and you see the difference between early valley thinking and later periods.

For seeing the Valley of the Kings as a change of era rather than a "beautiful tombs ranking," it is worth entering.

## The West Valley and Ay's tomb

The Valley of the Kings has the often-visited East Valley and the quieter West Valley.

The West Valley holds Ay's tomb. Ay took the throne after Tutankhamun's death. His tomb isn't usually built into standard tourism. Confirm at the time whether it is open and what the access conditions are.

For a first visit with limited time, the East Valley alone is enough. For a second Luxor trip or a deeper valley trip, the West Valley has value in its very quietness.

## How to combine

In the Valley of the Kings, the base is choosing three tombs on the standard ticket and, if needed, adding one or two supplementary-ticket tombs.

For first-timers, from the standard tombs, choose one large Ramesside tomb with strong colour. For example KV11 or KV2. Another, choose a tomb showing process or older style. If open, KV57 or KV34. Add KV9 supplementary, and KV17 if there is margin — a very strong visit.

Consider Tutankhamun separately. For art, KV17 and KV9 are strong. For story, KV62.

When unsure, choose "tombs that show something different" rather than "the most famous." Large tomb, striking-ceiling tomb, tomb with unfinished lines, small but historically important tomb. Combined that way, the valley reads as nearly five hundred years of royal burial change, not one underground museum.

## The visit in practice

The Valley of the Kings is a morning place.

[VERIFY: current opening hours, checked date] The first hour has lower temperature, fewer groups, and quieter tomb interiors. In summer especially, the later you go, the harsher the outside transfer.

Air inside tombs closes in. As people increase, heat and humidity shift the feel. Not only a comfort issue. It affects wall-painting preservation. Tombs open and close in rotation for conservation, not only for tourism.

Water, hat, walking shoes are needed. Little shade in the valley; walking between tombs alone uses stamina.

Photography rules change. Flash, tripods, video gear, professional equipment can be restricted. Even where phone photos are allowed, follow on-site signs and staff direction.

Don't touch walls. In narrow places, mind that bag or shoulder doesn't hit the wall. The paintings have lasted more than three thousand years but are weak to mass tourism.

## Tombs you can't see

Not everything in the Valley of the Kings is open.

KV5, built for Ramesses II's sons, is known as one of the largest tombs in the valley but isn't entered on normal visits. Investigation and conservation continue.

Some royal tombs are not yet fully identified. The Valley of the Kings looks fully discovered; in practice, it still holds questions.

This unfinished quality is part of the valley's appeal. The visible tombs alone are deep enough. Beneath and beyond them sits history not yet solved.

## In sum

What matters in the Valley of the Kings isn't adding count.

See the peak of art with Seti I. See ceiling and colour with Ramesses V/VI. Touch the archaeological story with Tutankhamun. Feel royal-tomb scale and legibility with Ramesses III or Ramesses IV. See process and early style with Horemheb or Thutmose III.

Chosen that way, the Valley of the Kings stops being "a collection of famous tombs."

It becomes the place where kings wrote into rock how they tried to pass death.
