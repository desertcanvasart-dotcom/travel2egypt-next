Tutankhamun's tomb isn't the largest in the [Valley of the Kings](/guide/luxor/valley-of-the-kings), nor the most magnificently decorated. Yet the small tomb known as KV62 played a decisive role in showing ancient Egypt to the world.

When Howard Carter found it in 1922, many of the valley's royal tombs had already been looted in antiquity. Tutankhamun's still held the king's grave goods in great quantity: the golden mask, coffins, shrines, chariots, a throne, jewellery, everyday objects and ritual items. The discovery shook not only archaeology but twentieth-century popular culture.

Tutankhamun himself wasn't a long-reigning, powerful king. His fame comes less from anything he achieved in life than from his tomb surviving nearly intact. That chance is what makes this tomb special.

## The Valley of the Kings and its hidden tombs

The Valley of the Kings is a dry valley on [Luxor](/guide/luxor)'s west bank. New Kingdom kings chose to hide their tombs in the mountain rather than build conspicuous pyramids, to deter looting.

Hiding them did not solve the problem. Many were still robbed in antiquity; even with sealed entrances, the treasures inside were taken in case after case. The larger and better-known a tomb was, the earlier it was found and stripped. Priests later moved royal mummies elsewhere to protect them.

Against that background, finding Tutankhamun's tomb with so much of its burial equipment still in place was very rare. There are traces of ancient intrusion, but the tomb was never emptied.

## Howard Carter's search

Howard Carter first came to Egypt as a painter. He began by copying wall paintings, and through long years on the Theban west bank he became an excavator who knew the Valley of the Kings exceptionally well.

He believed Tutankhamun's tomb had not yet been found. Small objects bearing the king's name and fragments linked to his burial supported that belief. Earlier excavators thought the valley had nothing major left to give; Carter didn't accept it.

With Lord Carnarvon's funding, he searched for years. With no results, there was talk of stopping. In what was treated as a final chance, re-examining a corner of the valley that many believed exhausted, his workers found a step in November 1922. It was the top of a stairway leading down to a sealed entrance, with another blocked entrance further in.

Weeks later, Carter and Carnarvon looked through a small hole in the sealed doorway. Candlelight fell on gilded couches, boxes, chariots, guardian figures and vessels. Carnarvon asked whether he could see anything, and Carter is said to have answered, "Yes, wonderful things."

The discovery was dramatic; the work that followed was longer and more mundane. Recording, photographing, conserving and removing the contents took roughly ten years.

## Records that changed archaeology

What matters about Carter's excavation isn't only the richness of the find. Before removing anything, his team photographed it, numbered it, recorded its position and noted its condition. That is standard practice now; for the time, it was very thorough.

Thanks to that record, KV62 is not simply "a tomb where treasure came out" but a tomb whose burial can be reconstructed. The objects survived, and so did the relationship between the objects and the space.

The tomb showed the public, in a form anyone could follow, archaeology moving from treasure hunting to a discipline built on recording.

## The tomb's structure

KV62 is small as royal tombs go. Its main spaces are the antechamber, the annex, the burial chamber and the treasury. Compared with the large Ramesside tombs nearby, both passages and rooms are modest.

The small scale is usually linked to Tutankhamun dying young and a burial that may have been prepared in haste. It has also been suggested that the tomb was originally meant for someone else and adapted for the king.

The decoration is mainly in the burial chamber. The walls show Tutankhamun on his way to the afterlife and the opening-of-the-mouth ritual. There is less decoration than in the large tombs, and the space carries the tension of a burial arranged quickly for a young king.

## What was in the tomb

Over five thousand items were packed into that small space. The golden mask is the most famous, but it is only one part of the whole.

The antechamber held animal-shaped couches, chariots, boxes, furniture, vessels and everyday goods. The treasury held the canopic chest, divine figures, model boats and ritual objects. The burial chamber held the nested shrines, the sarcophagus, the coffins and the king's mummy. Across the tomb there were bows, staves, clothing, shabtis and jewellery.

These things weren't treasure for its own sake. They were what the king was thought to need to go on living in the afterlife: food, clothing, weapons, furniture, amulets, statues and ritual equipment, placed to support him on the way.

Smaller pieces (chairs, sandals, clothes, games, hunting gear) show a side close to the king's daily life: a sacred ruler, and also a young man who actually lived. Where each object lay mattered as much as the object itself. Knowing what was in which room, and how the rooms were used, is what gives the tomb its value for understanding royal burial.

## Why the golden mask became famous

Tutankhamun's golden mask is one of the most famous objects of ancient Egypt. The idealised young face, the blue-and-gold stripes and the symbols of sacred kingship make a strong and lasting impression.

The mask wasn't made as art for people to look at. It was funerary equipment, meant to protect the king's mummy and help his transformation in the afterlife.

Through photographs and exhibitions in the modern era, it became better known than Tutankhamun himself, and came to stand for ancient Egypt as a whole. A king unremarkable in life became a global symbol through his burial goods.

## Why the tomb survived

There isn't one reason. The tomb was small, and its entrance was covered by debris from later work in the valley, which may have delayed its discovery.

Tutankhamun was also a king tied to the upheaval of the Amarna period, and later kings tended to erase his name from the record. Ironically, being forgotten drew attention away from him and helped preserve the tomb.

It was not a completely undisturbed tomb. But when so many tombs in the valley were nearly empty, finding this much burial equipment together is what made the discovery decisive.

## What kind of king was Tutankhamun?

Tutankhamun was a young king of the 18th Dynasty. He probably came to the throne at around age 9 and ruled for about ten years, dying in his late teens. His reign came after Akhenaten's religious reform, when the traditional cults, above all that of Amun, were being restored.

As a boy king, his political decisions were probably shaped heavily by powerful courtiers and officials. How far he led policy himself is a question to treat with care.

The cause of his death (illness, fracture, infection, inherited health problems) is still debated. Assassination has been suggested, but there is no decisive evidence. For travellers, it is enough to know that he died young and that his burial was probably arranged in a hurry.

## Egypt and the question of ownership

1922 mattered for Egyptian politics too. Egypt was moving from British influence towards formal independence, and who owned antiquities was not only an academic question. The era when foreign excavators took their finds abroad was changing.

Tutankhamun's grave goods stayed in Egypt. It was a clear expression of the idea that Egypt's cultural heritage belongs to Egypt.

## The curse, and what it isn't

The story of "the pharaoh's curse" is bound up with this tomb. One trigger was Lord Carnarvon's death soon after the opening; newspapers made it a sensation, and the idea spread that a curse fell on those who disturbed the tomb.

No famous curse text was written in the tomb. Carnarvon's death is thought to have been caused by an infection, at a time before antibiotics were widely available, when such infections could be fatal. Old tombs do carry real health risks (dust, mould, poor ventilation), but they are physical, not supernatural. We look at the legend in more detail in [the curse of King Tut's tomb](/blog/the-curse-of-king-tuts-tomb).

The curse is part of the tomb's modern reception, not its archaeology. What matters is how the tomb survived, how it was recorded and how the world received it.

## Worldwide Egypt fever

The discovery set off a worldwide wave of Egyptomania. Fashion, jewellery, architecture, film and advertising in the 1920s took up Egyptian-style design; the golden mask, cobras, wings, geometric patterns and pharaonic imagery merged with the Art Deco aesthetic.

The press made it possible. The find was covered at length in the newspapers and reached a world audience through photographs and stories. For the first time, an archaeological excavation became a mass-media event.

Tutankhamun's fame wasn't made by ancient greatness alone. The timing of the discovery, the way it was reported, the visual power of the gold and the story of an "almost intact royal tomb" all layered on top of each other.

## Fitting it into an itinerary

To follow Tutankhamun's story properly, split it between two places.

In Luxor, see KV62 in the Valley of the Kings. If you have time, add a larger, more decorated tomb such as KV17 or KV9, and KV62's smallness and its importance both become clearer.

In Giza, see the grave goods at the Grand Egyptian Museum. Beyond the golden mask, the furniture, chariots, jewellery, ritual items and near-everyday objects show how broad the tomb's contents were.

The order works either way. See the museum first, and the smallness of the tomb stays with you. See the tomb first, and the museum shows how much came out of those narrow rooms.

## Why it still matters

Tutankhamun wasn't Egypt's most powerful king, and his tomb is neither the largest nor the most lavishly decorated. What it did, in a form no other tomb has, was show what a New Kingdom royal burial looked like before looters reached it. Without it, our understanding of royal burial would be far more fragmentary.

The discovery shaped archaeology, museums, the debate over cultural ownership, popular culture and tourism. And it was chance: a small tomb, a hidden entrance, an excavator who didn't give up and twentieth-century media that carried the story round the world.

When you visit KV62, there is no need to be disappointed by its scale. That small space made Tutankhamun the most famous pharaoh in the world. Seeing it isn't only seeing ancient Egypt; it is also seeing how the modern world discovered, recorded, owned and imagined it.
