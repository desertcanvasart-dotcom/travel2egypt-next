This question needs less a short answer than a correct split. Someone travelling [Cairo](/guide/cairo), [Giza](/guide/giza), [Luxor](/guide/luxor), [Aswan](/guide/aswan), a Nile cruise and the Red Sea resorts and someone thinking about North Sinai or the Libyan border are facing completely different kinds of risk inside the same country.

On the standard tourist routes, Egypt is a country many travellers realistically travel. [VERIFY: 2025 visitor-arrivals figure and its source; the page previously said around 19 million] Airports, hotels, guides, drivers, Nile cruises and Red Sea resorts are used to handling foreign travellers.

But talking about Egypt as a whole at one safety level is a mistake. There are areas to avoid. There are situations to watch around political expression and photography. There are scams and persistent touts in the cities. Women travelling alone should be ready for unwanted attention and harassment. And heat, water, food and road transfers cause more real problems than most people expect.

Most of what actually goes wrong for travellers is not violent crime. It is touts, price negotiation, rough roads, heat, stomach trouble, places you may not photograph, and stares or comments aimed at women on their own. None of it should be taken lightly; almost all of it is manageable if you know about it in advance.

So Egypt is not a country to be afraid of. It is also not a country to move through knowing nothing.

## Think by region

The first thing to look at on an Egypt trip is not the country but the map.

Most travellers visit Cairo and Giza, Luxor and Aswan, [Abu Simbel](/guide/abu-simbel), [Alexandria](/guide/alexandria), the Nile cruise, [Hurghada](/guide/hurghada), [Marsa Alam](/guide/marsa-alam) and [Sharm el-Sheikh](/guide/sharm-el-sheikh). Visitors move through these places every day and the reception is organised. Around major sites and hotels, police and tourist police are visible; at airports, museums, temples and on parts of the road network there are checkpoints and bag checks.

North Sinai is different. [VERIFY: current government advisories for North Sinai, central Sinai, the Libyan and Sudanese border areas and the Western Desert, e.g. UK FCDO, US State Department, Australia Smartraveller] At the time of the last review, several governments advised against travel to North Sinai and issued strong cautions on border zones and parts of the desert. Areas close to the Libyan border, military-controlled desert and border zones are not places to treat as extensions of an ordinary holiday. This does not put Cairo and Luxor at the same risk level. It means that in Egypt, separating the places you can go from the places that are not for personal judgment is the foundation of safety.

South Sinai is considered separately from North Sinai. Sharm el-Sheikh, [Dahab](/guide/dahab) and St. Catherine receive visitors; inland Sinai and road transfers need checking by season and security situation. [VERIFY: current advisory position and road access for St. Catherine and inland South Sinai] For St. Catherine especially, travel on trusted arrangements and current local information.

[Siwa](/guide/siwa-oasis), [Bahariya](/guide/bahariya-oasis), the White Desert and the Fayoum reserves are rewarding desert destinations, but they are not organised like an afternoon in a museum. Vehicle, permits where required, checkpoints, phone signal and weather all matter. Do not enter remote desert without a trusted local arrangement.

## Cairo and Giza

The most common problem travellers meet in Cairo is not violent crime.

It is traffic chaos, persistent touts, overcharging, fake guides and being steered into tourist shops. Around the Giza pyramids, in [Khan el-Khalili](/guide/cairo/khan-el-khalili) and on the way out of the airport, the pressure can feel high for first-timers. Offers of camels, carriages, photos, guiding and souvenirs multiply; something called free turns out not to be, a short ride becomes a large bill, someone who looked official turns out to be nothing of the sort.

This is less danger than the tiring kind of risk. Arrange car and guide in advance. Agree fares before getting in. Don't accept guidance you didn't ask for. Decline briefly and clearly. Those four alone help a great deal.

In markets, the metro, stations and crowded sites, watch for pickpockets and bag snatching. Leave your original passport in the hotel safe unless you need it for a transfer, and carry a copy or a photo on your phone. Don't keep all your cash in one place. Don't walk with your phone in the hand on the traffic side. None of this is specific to Egypt; it is the basics of any large tourist city.

At Cairo's larger hotels, museums and major sites, security is visible. That is reassuring, and it is also a sign of how much weight Egypt puts on tourism.

## Luxor, Aswan and the Nile cruise

Luxor and Aswan are relatively easy cities to move through.

Luxor has many visitors, and so more touts. At temple entrances, ferry landings and parts of the west bank, people will approach you. Aswan is generally calmer; in the souks and at the boat landings the same exchanges happen.

The Nile cruise is one of Egypt's most established tourist corridors. The Luxor–[Edfu](/guide/edfu)–[Kom Ombo](/guide/kom-ombo)–Aswan route has run for a long time, with settled routines for boats, guides and drivers. For first-time visitors who want to reduce the load of transfers, it is a useful corridor.

The itineraries to watch are long road journeys through Middle Egypt. Minya, [Asyut](/guide/asyut) and [Sohag](/guide/sohag) hold important sites, but tourist infrastructure is thinner than in Luxor or Aswan, and road travel there can involve permits or a tourist-police escort. [VERIFY: current escort and permit requirements for Middle Egypt road travel] Travel with an operator who knows the local situation rather than putting it together loosely yourself; our piece on [police escorts in Egypt](/blog/police-escorts-in-egypt) explains how that system works.

## Getting around

A risk many travellers miss in Egypt is the road.

Cairo traffic is hard to read on arrival. Lanes, gaps between cars, crossings and horn use follow a local logic. On a short trip there is little point in driving yourself. In the cities, hotel-arranged cars, trusted drivers and ride-hailing apps are the realistic options. [VERIFY: Uber and Careem both currently operating in Cairo and other cities named]

For long distances, mix domestic flights, trains and private cars to suit the itinerary. Between Cairo and Luxor or Aswan, fly to save time or take the train if you want the journey itself. Road transfers to the Red Sea are common; avoid long-distance night driving where you can. On desert roads, rest stops, checkpoints, vehicle condition and the driver's experience tie directly to safety.

The Cairo metro is practical and has women-only carriages. It can feel tense the first time, but for moving around the centre it avoids the road traffic.

## Red Sea and water safety

In Hurghada, [El Gouna](/guide/al-gouna), Soma Bay, Marsa Alam and Sharm el-Sheikh, safety is less about walking the town and more about water and transfers.

Hotels and dive centres are used to foreign travellers, and at the larger resorts even first-timers find things straightforward. But the sea is not a managed pool. Current, wind, boats, depth, coral, certification and experience all come into it.

For snorkelling and diving, use licensed, reputable operators. Sea conditions, current, the boat's safety management, guide ratios and whether oxygen is carried are not visible from the price. Even snorkelling, stay inside marked areas, and don't go to sites beyond your certification or experience. Good swimmers too should not overrate themselves on offshore reefs, open water or current-prone sites.

Don't stand on coral, don't touch marine life, don't wreck your buoyancy for a photo. That protects the reef and you. When a beach is closed or swimming is forbidden, follow the instruction rather than ask why.

If you head into town, leave the resort mindset behind. Don't walk local shopping streets in swimwear, and treat transport and fares with the same care as in Cairo.

## Scams and touts

The most common unpleasant experience on an Egypt trip is less a scam than pressured tourist commerce.

"It's closed here." "The ticket office is this way." "Free guiding." "I'll take your photo." "This camel is cheap." Most of these openers lead to a fee or a shop in the end. For the full picture, see our guide to [touts in Egypt](/travel-tips/touts-in-egypt).

The most effective response is not a vague laugh. Decline briefly — "la, shukran" — and keep walking; start explaining and the conversation continues. Set prices first, and don't get on a camel or horse until price and time are agreed. Confirm tickets only at official windows or on the official site. For a guide or a car, use someone selected in advance, not someone who suddenly approaches you on the spot.

This does not mean Egyptians are unkind. In ordinary shops, hotels, family restaurants, on trains and by the roadside, many people will help you. Around the entrances of tourist sites, an economy built to extract the most from visitors sits on top of that. Telling the two apart matters.

## Women travellers and families

Egypt is not a country where women can't travel alone. It is not an easy country to walk, either.

Women on their own should expect stares, comments, requests for photos and pushy invitations to talk. Most of it is words and attitude, but it accumulates and tires you. Clothing does not prevent it entirely; covering shoulders and knees, carrying a scarf for mosques, avoiding quiet streets late at night, using ride-hailing or hotel cars after dark and taking the women-only metro carriages all lower the load. If something feels wrong, leave. Our [guide for solo women travellers in Egypt](/travel-tips/solo-woman-traveler-in-egypt) goes into more detail.

At resorts, don't drop your guard completely. Hotels and tours are comfortable, but don't be alone with people you have just met, don't leave drinks unattended, and don't leave children solely with hotel or excursion staff.

Families are warmly received. Children get a lot of attention and people help when you are stuck. Sites are not pushchair-friendly: sand, steps, narrow tomb passages and few handrails mean small children need a carrier or close adult watch. Child seats in cars are not standard; arrange one in advance if you need it.

## Politics, photography and the law

In Egypt, political topics and photography need care.

Sites, streetscapes, food, the Nile and desert landscapes are usually fine. Don't photograph military personnel, police, checkpoints, military or government facilities, bridges, canals, airports or security buildings. The [Suez](/guide/suez) Canal and bridges can be treated more strictly than a traveller would expect. This is not a light matter of manners: it can lead to questioning or detention. If there are demonstrations or political gatherings, don't go near and don't photograph. When in doubt, don't take the picture.

Drones are not something to bring in casually and fly. Unauthorised import or use leads to confiscation or worse. For filming or news work, confirm formal authorisation before you travel.

Be careful on social media too. Within ordinary sightseeing, problems are uncommon, but posting strong criticism of the political system or the security agencies while inside Egypt is an unnecessary risk. Penalties for illegal drugs are very heavy, even for small amounts, and public drunkenness is not something to take lightly.

For LGBTQ+ travellers, Egypt calls for legal and social caution. Don't judge only on whether a law explicitly bans same-sex relations: public-morality laws have been used, and public expression carries real risk. Take care with public affection and with dating apps. For any couple, restraint with public intimacy is realistic.

## Health, water and heat

For most travellers, what actually causes problems is water, food and heat.

Don't drink tap water; use bottled water, ideally for brushing teeth too. Eating from stalls or small places is not bad in itself, but be careful with food that has sat in the heat, salads or fruit possibly washed in untreated water, and ice. Food served hot, from busy places with fast turnover, is the safer choice.

Before you go, see a travel clinic or your doctor about vaccinations for your age, health, length of stay and destinations. [VERIFY: current CDC or equivalent vaccine guidance for Egypt; the source post cited hepatitis A, hepatitis B and typhoid]

The heat in Luxor, Aswan and Abu Simbel shapes the itinerary. In hot months, see sites early and rest in the middle of the day. Hat, sunscreen, rehydration salts and plenty of water are not luxuries in summer Upper Egypt; they are health management.

Cairo has private hospitals used by foreign travellers. The Red Sea resorts, Luxor and Aswan have medical facilities, but specialisation and equipment vary. Serious illness or injury can mean evacuation, so make sure your travel insurance includes medical evacuation, and check that diving, desert trips or trekking are covered if you plan them. Our [health and safety guide](/travel-tips/health-and-safety) covers the practical side in more depth.

## What changes with the season

The risks above don't stay constant through the year.

From late spring to early autumn, heat in Luxor, Aswan, Abu Simbel and the desert becomes the main factor: move early, rest at midday, and don't plan long outdoor stretches around noon. On the Red Sea, wind and current change sea conditions, and beach closures follow them. Desert trips depend on weather as much as on the route. ## Emergency numbers

Numbers to keep on your phone in Egypt: [VERIFY: emergency numbers still current]

- Police: 122
- Ambulance: 123
- Fire: 180
- Tourist police: 126

For theft, scams, trouble at a site or a serious dispute with a tout, the tourist police are the first place to go, and they are present at major sites. Language and speed on the phone vary, so asking your hotel, guide or cruise staff for help at the same time is realistic.

Save your own embassy's contact details before you leave. A lost passport, an arrest, an accident or a hospital stay is not something a tour operator or hotel can close on its own.

## Check before you go

Always check your own government's travel advice before booking and again before leaving: the UK FCDO, the US State Department and Australia's Smartraveller, among others. These show risk by region, not only for the country as a whole.

Don't decide only on a tour operator's or hotel's "it's fine." Equally, don't decide on one frightening story on social media. Current official advice, the working knowledge of the people arranging your trip on the ground, and your actual itinerary: put those three together and the judgment becomes realistic.

## In sum

Egypt is a country many travellers can travel safely.

That doesn't mean equally safe everywhere. Avoid North Sinai and the border zones. Understand the commercial pressure at Giza and in the markets. If you are a woman on your own, allow a little margin in transport and clothing. Avoid political photos and statements. Watch the heat and the water. Don't choose boats, cars or excursions on price alone.

A good itinerary is itself a safety measure. Don't push hard on your first day in Cairo. At Giza, arrange entrance, tickets, car and guide in advance. In Luxor, avoid the hot hours. Do the desert as an arranged trip. At night, decide in advance where you will walk and where you will take a car.

Safety isn't believing nothing will happen.

It is knowing where things tend to happen, and being able to move calmly with that knowledge.
