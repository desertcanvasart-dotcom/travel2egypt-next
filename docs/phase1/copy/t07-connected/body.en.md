## Staying Connected in Egypt

Until quite recently, staying connected in Egypt meant something specific. It meant payphones, hotel landlines, prepaid international cards, internet cafés, and the small ritual of dialling country codes through three layers of operators while a hotel receptionist sighed beside you. None of that is what connectivity in Egypt looks like now. The country runs on smartphones, on four mobile networks with thorough 4G coverage and expanding 5G, on a hotel-and-café Wi-Fi infrastructure that meets international expectations across most of the country, on WhatsApp for almost everything that once required a phone call, and increasingly on eSIMs that arrive in a traveller's phone before the traveller does. The internet cafés have closed. The dial-up era is over. The framing that Egypt is a place where connectivity is uncertain is roughly a decade out of date.

For most visitors, the practical questions are straightforward: which SIM or eSIM, which network, how much data, what to expect from hotel and café Wi-Fi, and how to handle the few places where coverage genuinely thins. The answers depend on the kind of trip — a Nile cruise, a desert expedition, a work-from-Cairo month, and a family week on the Red Sea all make different demands on a connection.

This is a practical guide to all of it.

## The Mobile Networks

Four mobile operators serve Egypt, three of them globally familiar and one less so.

Vodafone Egypt is the largest, with the strongest coverage outside the major cities and the most extensive rural and desert reach. For travellers heading to the Western Desert oases, the White Desert, parts of Sinai's interior, or smaller towns in Upper Egypt, Vodafone tends to be the safest single-network choice.

Orange Egypt, formerly Mobinil before its 2016 rebrand under the French Orange group, is the second-largest. Coverage in the cities and along the Nile corridor is excellent, and some travellers prefer Orange for slightly better data speeds in central Cairo and Alexandria.

Etisalat by e&, the Egyptian arm of the UAE-based e& telecom group, is the third major operator. Coverage in cities and tourist areas is comparable to the other two; its rural reach is slightly weaker.

WE is Telecom Egypt's mobile arm, launched in 2017 to give the state-owned national fixed-line carrier a mobile presence. It is the newest of the four and has grown rapidly. Coverage in Cairo, Alexandria, and the major tourist cities is now solid; in remote areas it remains the patchiest of the four.

For most travellers, the practical choice comes down to Vodafone or Orange. Both have airport kiosks, extensive city-centre stores, and tourist-friendly data packages. Etisalat and WE are perfectly fine in cities, and less ideal for long-distance and remote travel.

## Getting a SIM Card

Any visitor planning to be in Egypt longer than a few days will save money and frustration with a local SIM. Roaming charges from foreign carriers remain expensive for non-EU travellers, and even within the EU's roaming-included plans, Egypt is not part of the standard "Europe" packages — it sits in the more expensive "World" zone.

The airports are the most convenient place to buy. Cairo International, Hurghada, Sharm El Sheikh, Luxor, and Aswan all have operator kiosks in the arrivals halls, often open to match major flight times. Prices are very slightly higher than in a city-centre store, but the convenience of being connected as you leave the airport is worth the difference. If you arrive late or want to compare offers, Vodafone, Orange, Etisalat, and WE all have official stores in central Cairo, along the Corniche in Alexandria, and in the tourist strips of Luxor, Aswan, Hurghada, and Sharm El Sheikh.

A passport is required to register any SIM in Egypt. The shop will scan or photocopy it and register the SIM to your name; this is an anti-fraud measure applied uniformly across all operators. Note that Orange Egypt's own eSIM page states tourists are eligible for a new line while a separate answer on the same page requires "valid residence" — the two statements contradict each other, so confirm directly with the shop which entry documents they'll accept before counting on this route. Bring the passport itself, not a photocopy — the staff need to see the original.

Tourist SIM packages typically run EGP 200–400, with the higher end usually including significantly more data — often 30 GB or more — and sometimes a small allotment of voice minutes. Local-only SIMs without a bundled tourist package can be cheaper, but they require separate top-ups for data, which is more complicated for a short stay. Operators reshuffle their tourist packages periodically, so confirm the current bundle and data allotment at the kiosk on arrival rather than relying on a fixed quote from older travel writing. One small note: Egyptian SIMs are sold in nano-SIM form by default, and older phones requiring a full-size or micro-SIM may need an adapter, which most kiosks can provide.

Test the line before you leave the counter. Insert the SIM, restart the phone, open a map or a browser, and wait until data is actually moving. An activation problem takes minutes to sort out at the kiosk and a great deal longer from a hotel room the next day.

Ask what the bundle actually contains: the data allowance, how long it is valid, whether local calls are included and international calls charged separately, whether the price includes tax, whether tethering is allowed, and how to top up. Tourist bundles sold as "lots of data" are sometimes split between social-media, video, and general data; the number that matters is how much general data, usable in any app, the bundle includes. Keep the receipt and the card the SIM came on. They may carry the number and package details, and the shop will want them if something needs fixing later.

## eSIM Options

For travellers with eSIM-compatible phones — most iPhones from 2018 onwards, most flagship Android phones from 2020 onwards — the eSIM route avoids the airport kiosk entirely.

Several international eSIM providers sell Egypt packages that activate immediately on arrival. Airalo and Holafly are the most widely used, with Saily, Nomad, and Roamless offering competitive plans. Two providers, Airalo and Holafly, sell "unlimited" Egypt data. Airalo's unlimited plans run $32 for 7 days or $35 for 10 days, but its fair-use policy throttles speed to 1 Mbps after 3 GB in a 24-hour window unless the specific package states otherwise. Holafly's terms allow a comparable temporary slowdown under its network partners' policies. Neither should be mistaken for the kind of connection you'd use to tether a laptop for a full workday — if you need real bandwidth, buy data by the gigabyte rather than trusting the word "unlimited."

Prices move; confirm the current rate before buying. Coverage depends on which Egyptian network the eSIM connects to; most international providers piggyback on Vodafone Egypt or Orange Egypt, with broadly similar real-world coverage.

Native Egyptian carrier eSIMs are increasingly available too. Vodafone Egypt and Orange Egypt both now support eSIM activation through their apps and official stores, though the in-store process can be slower than a physical-SIM purchase because of the digital provisioning steps. For a longer stay, or for anyone wanting a local number, an in-country eSIM from Vodafone or Orange is worth considering.

The eSIM advantage is straightforward: no airport queue, no shop visit, no passport scanning, immediate connectivity on arrival. The disadvantages are cost per gigabyte and the inability to share or transfer the SIM between devices.

Whichever route you choose, check before departure that the phone is unlocked. A phone still locked to its home carrier will reject an Egyptian SIM, and a carrier lock can block an eSIM even on a compatible handset. For an international eSIM, read the activation steps before leaving and keep the QR code or app details available offline; you may need them before you have any connection at all.

Travellers who need their home number, for bank SMS codes, two-factor authentication, or calls to a work line, can keep the home SIM in the phone for calls and texts and run data on an Egyptian SIM or eSIM. Many recent smartphones take a physical SIM and an eSIM together; confirm that yours does before relying on it. Home-network roaming is then best kept as a backup for the first hour after landing, until the local line works, rather than as the trip's main connection.

## Coverage and Speed

Egypt's 4G network is mature and works well across the country. Every major city, every significant archaeological site, the intercity highways, the Red Sea coast, the Mediterranean coast, and most agricultural Nile Valley areas have reliable 4G from at least one of the four operators. Mobile 4G speeds typically run 20–50 Mbps, and 5G — rolling out across Cairo, Alexandria, the New Administrative Capital, and the largest Red Sea resorts — can reach 200 Mbps and beyond in coverage areas. For most travellers, 5G is not yet a planning consideration; 4G is more than sufficient for navigation, messaging, video calls, and streaming.

On the fixed-line side, average broadband runs 25–100 Mbps in the residential and commercial areas of the major cities, with upper-tier hotels and co-working spaces delivering 100–300 Mbps. The global rankings place Egypt in the middle of the table — better than several regional peers, behind the Gulf states' high-end infrastructure. Streaming, uploads, and standard video-conferencing are comfortably handled; large file transfers, such as raw video or high-resolution photo batches, work but run slower than European or East Asian norms.

Coverage weakens in a few predictable places. The Western Desert beyond the populated oases of Bahariya, Farafra, Dakhla, and Kharga can have substantial dead zones. Parts of Sinai's interior, particularly the mountain trails around Saint Catherine's, lose signal entirely in places. The smaller Red Sea islands, the deeper reaches of the Sahara on a White Desert overnight, and a few quiet stretches of the Sa'idi countryside can also drop to weak signal or nothing. For most travellers this matters in only two situations: desert excursions, where a satellite phone or a local guide arrangement matters more than a mobile signal, and the few minutes before and after a flight, when you might assume airport Wi-Fi is universal and find that it is not.

Long desert roads follow the same pattern. Expect gaps on the Luxor–Hurghada road, on transfers between the oases, and on the road to Siwa, and save maps, tickets, and booking confirmations offline before setting out.

## Hotel Wi-Fi

Hotel Wi-Fi in Egypt is mostly a question of category and brand standardisation.

The international chains — Hilton, Sofitel, Marriott, Four Seasons, Conrad, Mövenpick, Steigenberger, Kempinski, Hyatt, Le Méridien, and the other major brands listed in the tips-on-accommodations guide — universally offer complimentary Wi-Fi for guests, with speeds and reliability matching their brand-global standards. At the upper tier in Cairo, Alexandria, Luxor, Aswan, and the Red Sea resort cities, speeds typically run 50–200 Mbps with stable coverage in rooms and public areas. The premium properties — the Four Seasons Cairo at Nile Plaza, Marriott Mena House, Sofitel Cairo Nile El Gezirah, Conrad Cairo, the Hilton Luxor Resort & Spa — reliably deliver video-streaming and large-file-upload performance.

Mid-range Egyptian hotels typically offer Wi-Fi at the entry-level broadband tier: usable for messaging, email, and basic browsing, sometimes limited for video. The quality of the connection — a single property-wide network, password-protected, with sensible bandwidth allocation — varies, and the tips-on-accommodations guide covers the broader category-by-category framing.

Boutique and heritage hotels vary widely. The Sofitel Legend Old Cataract in Aswan, the historic Steigenberger Cecil in Alexandria, and the larger heritage properties operate at international-chain standards. Smaller boutique places and West Bank Luxor guesthouses can have functional-but-limited connections — fine for messaging and email, not always for video work.

Nile cruise ships are variable. Newer ships in the Mövenpick, Sonesta, and Steigenberger fleets offer functional cabin Wi-Fi, though the connection on a moving river is inherently more limited than on land. Dahabiya sailing vessels have minimal or no Wi-Fi by design, which is rather the point of them.

The takeaway: at the upper tiers, the question of whether hotel Wi-Fi is good has been settled. Where it matters is at the mid-range and below, where a video call from a three-star Cairo hotel may work and may not. For anyone depending on a reliable connection for work or family calls, the upper-tier international chains are the safest single choice.

## Cafés, Public Networks, and Co-Working

The Egyptian café scene has matured into one of the most reliable alternatives to a hotel room. In Cairo, the chains — Starbucks, Costa Coffee, Cilantro (the largest Egyptian-owned chain), Coffee Republic, and others — run consistent networks at most branches, generally adequate for video calls, uploads, and standard work. The Zamalek, Maadi, Heliopolis, and New Cairo branches are particularly reliable; central downtown branches can slow at peak times. The international fast-food chains — McDonald's, KFC, Pizza Hut, Cinnabon — offer Wi-Fi at almost every location, fine for everyday use but not for video work. The specialty cafés in the upscale neighbourhoods often beat the chains: places like Coffee Bean & Tea Leaf, Doppio, Beano's, and a long list of independents in Maadi, Zamalek, and Heliopolis have become informal remote-work hubs over the past five years, several with laptop-friendly tables and reliable connections through the working day.

In Alexandria, the Corniche cafés and the old Downtown coffee houses — Caffe Trianon, trading since 1907; the Brazilian Coffee Stores, since 1929; Athineos — all offer Wi-Fi of varying quality, and the Mediterranean resort strip has it at most cafés and restaurants in the resort zones. In Luxor and Aswan, café Wi-Fi concentrates at the hotels and the main tourist-area restaurants; the East Bank Corniche cafés are functional, while the West Bank guesthouses and smaller traditional cafés are more variable.

Public Wi-Fi at the airports has improved substantially. Cairo International Terminals 1 and 3 offer free access with phone-number sign-in, and Hurghada, Sharm El Sheikh, Luxor, and Aswan do the same — functional for messaging and basic browsing, slow for content work. On the Cairo Metro, the newer Lines 2 and 3 offer some access at major interchange stations; the older Line 1 rarely does. The larger shopping malls — Mall of Egypt, Cairo Festival City, City Stars, Mall of Arabia — provide complimentary Wi-Fi for visitors. One point worth stating plainly: public networks in Egypt, as anywhere, are not the place for sensitive transactions without a VPN. Banking, password logins, and confidential communication belong on personal mobile data or a properly secured connection, not on an open public network.

For anyone working from Egypt for weeks or months, co-working has been one of the more visible commercial trends of the post-2020 period. Cairo's market has expanded from a handful of locations in 2015 to several dozen by 2026. The major operators include The GrEEK Campus — Egypt's longest-established tech-and-co-working hub, in the former American University in Cairo buildings near Tahrir Square — along with District 5 in Maadi, The Workplace and AhEAD Coworking across Maadi, New Cairo, and Heliopolis, KMT House in Maadi, Almaqarr in Sheikh Zayed and elsewhere, Spotted Studio in Zamalek, and a number of smaller operations. Day passes typically run the equivalent of 10–25 US dollars, with weekly and monthly passes widely available, and Wi-Fi at the major spaces runs 50–200 Mbps. The time zone — EET, or EEST when daylight saving is observed — works for both European and Asian video meetings, with the Cairo workday overlapping the European afternoon and the Asian morning conveniently, and the cost of living is favourable against most Western capitals. The government introduced a remote-work visa pathway in 2022, though uptake has been modest; most digital nomads operate on the standard tourist visa — the 30-day visa-on-arrival or e-Visa covered in the passport-and-visa guide — extending through the government immigration offices in Cairo if needed. The limitation is geographic: this infrastructure is concentrated, almost entirely, in Cairo.

## VPNs, Streaming, and What's Accessible

The Egyptian internet carries some content filtering, primarily targeting political opposition sites and certain news outlets blocked from local IPs since the 2017–2019 actions, and historically some VoIP services. The major social platforms — Facebook, Instagram, X, TikTok, YouTube, WhatsApp, Telegram — are all accessible without a VPN, and the major streaming services operate with regional catalogues.

VPN use itself sits in a grey zone: the government has expressed concerns about it, but in practice the traffic is widely tolerated and not enforced against tourists. Most of the major commercial VPNs — NordVPN, ExpressVPN, ProtonVPN, Surfshark, Mullvad — function in Egypt, though some have shown intermittent connection trouble during certain political-event periods. For travellers wanting their home-country streaming libraries, a VPN remains the standard approach. And for verifying whether a specific site or service works from Egypt at all, the simplest check is a Wi-Fi browsing session before relying on it for anything important.

## How Egypt Actually Communicates

Two practical realities shape communication in Egypt that are not obvious from outside.

The first is that WhatsApp is the default messaging and voice-call platform across the country. Restaurants take reservations through it. Tour operators send confirmations through it. Felucca captains coordinate pick-ups through it. Small hotels handle bookings through it. Many businesses, especially smaller ones, are reachable through WhatsApp far more easily than through phone calls or email. A visitor who arrives without WhatsApp installed will find communication noticeably harder; with it, almost everything becomes simpler.

The second is that voice and video calls over data work fine on all four Egyptian networks. Earlier in the 2010s, Egypt periodically restricted VoIP on mobile data, which complicated travellers' use of WhatsApp calls, FaceTime, Skype, Zoom, and similar services. Those restrictions have not been consistently enforced in recent years, and voice and video calls over Egyptian mobile data are now reliable. The occasional dropped call usually has more to do with local signal strength than with any government-level interference.

For calling home, the cheapest option is almost always WhatsApp or FaceTime over local data. Direct international dialling from an Egyptian SIM is technically possible but expensive — a few minutes to most destinations can cost more than a full day's data package — and hotel landline calls are not worth the rate at any property. For a long conversation with someone who is not on WhatsApp or another VoIP platform, a callback service or virtual-number app such as Google Voice or Viber Out is the cheapest practical option. Skype, long the default for this purpose, was retired by Microsoft in May 2025 and is no longer available.

## Allocating Your Connectivity

For most travellers, the real question is how to divide connectivity between hotel Wi-Fi, café Wi-Fi, and mobile data.

The basic logic is straightforward. Hotel Wi-Fi handles the evening and overnight needs — streaming, video calls, uploads, working at the desk in the room. Café Wi-Fi handles the mid-day breaks — messaging, social, light work between sightseeing. And mobile data, via Egyptian SIM or eSIM, handles everything in between: walking through Khan el-Khalili needing a map, navigating between sites in Luxor, replying to a message from a felucca on the Nile, calling home from an airport gate.

For longer or work-intensive stays, the right answer is usually a layered one: hotel Wi-Fi as the primary working connection, free and fast and reliable at the upper tiers; an Egyptian SIM or eSIM for mobile data while moving around the country; café Wi-Fi as a flexible daytime alternative; and a co-working membership if the trip is long and work-heavy enough to justify it. That combination removes nearly all the friction.

The single biggest mistake is relying entirely on hotel Wi-Fi with no mobile data at all. It works in the hotel and at the major-site visitor centres, and much less well during the long hours spent moving between places — which, on most Egypt itineraries, is where the hours actually go.

## Practical Realities

Topping up data is easy. All four operators have apps with English-language interfaces that allow top-ups by credit card or local payment, in-store top-ups are straightforward, and tens of thousands of small shops across Egypt sell prepaid recharge cards in EGP 10, 25, 50, and 100 denominations.

Number portability between Egyptian operators exists but is not relevant for short-stay travellers; for longer-term visitors or expatriates, the option to keep a number while switching carriers is worth knowing. A lost or stolen phone can be reported to the operator, which will block the SIM, but reissuing a SIM on the same number generally requires a return visit to a store with a passport — for travellers, the simpler approach if a phone is lost is usually to buy a new SIM rather than recover the old one. Egypt's telecom regulator confirmed in January 2026 that tourists' phones keep a 90-day registration exemption; anyone staying longer, or returning with a device already used in Egypt before, should check the current registration process rather than assume the exemption still applies. For reference, the country code for incoming calls to Egypt is +20. Cairo landlines begin with 02, Alexandria with 03, and Luxor and Aswan with 095 and 097 respectively. Egyptian mobile numbers begin with 010, 011, 012, or 015, depending on which network the number was originally issued on — though portability has blurred the historic operator-prefix associations.

To call Egypt from abroad, dial your own international access code (00 from the UK, Ireland, and New Zealand; 011 from the US and Canada; 0011 from Australia) or simply +, then 20, then the area code or mobile prefix without its leading 0. A Cairo landline written 02 xxxx xxxx becomes +20 2 xxxx xxxx; a mobile written 010 xxxx xxxx becomes +20 10 xxxx xxxx. Cairo landline numbers have eight digits after the 02. To call out of Egypt, dial 00, then the country code (44 for the UK, 1 for the US and Canada, 61 for Australia), then the number.

## Final Word

Egypt has been wired to the world for longer than most travellers assume, but the way it is wired has changed almost completely in the past decade. The country talks to itself on WhatsApp now. It registers SIM cards by passport, sells eSIMs through Cairo Airport kiosks, runs 4G across the Western Desert, pings the New Administrative Capital with 5G, and streams video off a café balcony in Maadi about as well as a café in London would. The internet cafés have closed and the international-dialling card has gone the way of the previous century.

For most visitors, the only real decision left is which of four operators to buy a SIM from, and which venue suits the work — and even those matter less than the simple fact of being connected the moment you land.
