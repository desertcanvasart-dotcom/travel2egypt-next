Cairo is not a city you simply visit.

It is a city you enter by layers: pharaonic memory in museums and on the western edge of the metropolis, Roman walls beneath Coptic churches, Fatimid gates holding the old city line, Mamluk minarets rising over markets, Ottoman houses turned into museums, colonial boulevards, flyovers, coffeehouses, apartment blocks, river islands, desert roads, and the constant sound of a capital that has never stopped expanding.

Cairo overwhelms because it is not one city. It is several cities pressed into the same place.

## Why Cairo Matters

Cairo is Egypt's political, cultural, religious, and practical centre. It is the place where most journeys begin, but it should not be treated only as an arrival hall for pyramids or flights south.

Historic Cairo is a UNESCO World Heritage area. Coptic Cairo preserves some of the oldest Christian monuments in Egypt. Islamic Cairo holds one of the richest concentrations of medieval architecture in the world. Downtown Cairo and the Nile tell the story of the modern city. The land around the modern metropolis has been settled since antiquity — Memphis lay about 24 kilometres south at what is now Mit Rahina, and Heliopolis stood on ground that is today the suburb of Ain Shams — while the Roman fortress of Babylon, whose towers still rise in Coptic Cairo, anchored the settlement through the early Christian centuries.

The city that bears the name Cairo was founded in AD 969 by the Fatimid dynasty as al-Qahirah, "the Vanquisher," after the position of Mars at the moment of its foundation. The Fatimid Caliph Al-Mu'izz entered in AD 973, and under successive Ayyubid, Mamluk, and Ottoman rulers the city grew into the preeminent urban centre of the Islamic world — the inheritance that earned Cairo the epithet "city of a thousand minarets."

The city is difficult, loud, crowded, and often exhausting. It is also inexhaustible.

## The Main Areas

Islamic Cairo is the great walking landscape: Al-Muizz Street, Khan el-Khalili, Al-Azhar, Bab Zuweila, the Citadel, Ibn Tulun, Sultan Hassan, and the Northern Cemetery.

Coptic Cairo centres on the Babylon Fortress, the Hanging Church, Abu Serga, Saint Barbara, Ben Ezra Synagogue, and the Coptic Museum.

Downtown Cairo holds Tahrir Square, the Egyptian Museum, nineteenth and twentieth-century architecture, cafes, cinemas, bookshops, and the afterlife of khedival Cairo. ## Al-Azhar

Al-Azhar, founded around AD 970 to 972 by the Fatimids, is one of the oldest universities in the world and today the leading centre of Sunni Islamic scholarship globally. It did not adopt formal degree-granting university status until 1961, when it was nationalised and expanded to include secular faculties, but its more than a thousand years of continuous operation as a centre of learning make it a foundational institution of Islamic civilisation.

## Getting Here and Getting Around

Cairo International Airport sits about 22 kilometres north-east of the city centre and connects Cairo to most major global destinations. Uber and Careem operate from the airport, and official taxis are available at fixed rates from the transport desks. Trains connect Cairo to Alexandria (roughly two to two and a half hours, with frequent departures), Luxor, and Aswan via the Nile Valley line.

The Cairo Metro was the first metro system built in Africa and the Middle East, opening in 1987. It now runs three lines across 84 stations and roughly 107 kilometres of track, carrying around four million passengers a day — one of the highest ridership rates per kilometre of any system in the world. Cars 4 and 5 on each train are reserved for women, though women may also travel in general carriages. The metro is the fastest way to move between Tahrir Square, Ramses Station, Giza, and Helwan, but it does not directly serve the Giza Plateau, Islamic Cairo, or Khan el-Khalili. Uber and Careem are widely used and generally reliable; street taxis are everywhere — agree a fare before departing or insist on the meter.

## How Long To Stay

Two days covers only the essentials. Three to four days gives Cairo room to breathe: one day for Islamic Cairo, one for Coptic Cairo and the museums, one for the Citadel and Downtown, and one for Giza or Saqqara if those are not handled separately.

A week in Cairo is not excessive. It is the first honest amount of time.

## Practical Notes

Cairo is more cosmopolitan than provincial Egypt but still a conservative city by international standards. Modest dress is appropriate, particularly when visiting mosques, Coptic churches, and local markets. Women travelling alone should be aware that street harassment is common in crowded areas, and that the metro's women-only carriages offer a more comfortable option.

The Egyptian pound is the local currency, and cash is widely required. ATMs are available at airports, major hotels, and most urban bank branches. Tipping — baksheesh — is a normal part of economic life across the city.

Cairo's air pollution is significant and worth noting for travellers with respiratory conditions, particularly in winter when thermal inversion traps particulates over the city. Traffic congestion is severe at most hours; build in generous travel time between sites.

The best months to visit are October to April. Summers are hot and humid. Ramadan brings a significant shift in the city's rhythms: some restaurants close during daylight hours, but the nights become festive and the city takes on a different character that many visitors find worth experiencing.

## Final Word

Cairo is rarely easy.

But easy cities do not carry this much history in the open. Cairo's greatness lies in collision: sacred and ordinary, ancient and improvised, beautiful and broken, exhausting and alive.

Come prepared.

Then let the city be larger than your plan.
