Siwa feels less like a place you reach than a place the desert allows you to find.

It lies deep in Egypt's Western Desert, roughly 560 kilometres west of Cairo and about 50 kilometres from the Libyan border. For most of the journey there is only road, stone, sand, sky, and distance. Then the desert drops into a depression below sea level, and the oasis appears: palms, olives, salt lakes, springs, mud-brick ruins, and the pale edge of the Great Sand Sea.

Siwa is not simply another Egyptian oasis.

It is a Berber-speaking community of around 25,000 to 36,000 people with its own language, architecture, customs, embroidery, silverwork, wedding traditions, and social codes. It is Egyptian, but not Nile Valley Egyptian. It is desert, but not empty. It is ancient, but not frozen. Its isolation shaped a culture unlike anywhere else in the country.

People come for the Oracle of Amun, Alexander the Great, the ruins of Shali, the salt lakes, Cleopatra's Spring, desert safaris, and palm-framed sunsets.

They often remember something quieter.

The feeling of being very far from the centre of things.

## Where Siwa Is

Siwa sits in Matrouh Governorate, in a desert depression west of the Nile Valley and south of the Mediterranean coast.

The closest major coastal city is Marsa Matruh, reached by road across the desert. Cairo is much farther: around eight to ten hours by private car and 10 to 12 hours on the overnight bus, depending on route, stops, traffic and security procedures. There is no airport and no railway. The oasis covers around 1,018 square kilometres and reaches roughly 18 to 20 metres below sea level at its lowest points.

Most travellers come by road via Marsa Matruh, by public bus or private car, and some break the journey with a night on the coast. Routes, timings and the options from Cairo and Alexandria are in [How to Get to Siwa Oasis](/guide/siwa-oasis/how-to-go-in-siwa-oasis).

This distance matters.

Siwa should not be treated as a quick side trip. The journey is part of the destination, and the oasis rewards travellers who stay long enough for the road fatigue to fade.

Two full nights are the minimum. Three or four are much better.

## Shali Fortress

Shali is the visual heart of Siwa.

Its ruined walls rise in the centre of town, built from kershef, the traditional Siwan material made from salt-rich mud, clay, and local earth. Shali was founded in 1203 as a fortified settlement, not merely a castle. It was the town itself: houses, mosques, granaries, wells, lanes, courtyards, and defensive walls all compressed into a dense protected structure.

Kershef is perfectly suited to desert heat.

It is also vulnerable to rain.

In 1926, three days of heavy rainfall severely damaged Shali and accelerated its abandonment as families moved into newer houses outside the walls. The ruins have since become Siwa's most recognisable landmark, partly restored and stabilised through a 2018–2020 conservation project and reopened in 2020.

Climb toward the upper levels near sunset if access is safe.

From above, the oasis opens out: palms, salt lakes, low mountains, desert, and the modern town wrapped around its old core.

## The Oracle of Amun

The Temple of the Oracle stands at Aghurmi, a short distance from Siwa town.

It was one of the most famous oracles of the ancient Mediterranean. Built and developed around the cult of Amun, it drew rulers, generals, travellers, and seekers of legitimacy from far beyond the oasis. The temple was built during the reign of Amasis of the 26th Dynasty (570 to 526 BC).

Its most famous visitor was Alexander the Great.

In 331 BC, after conquering Egypt, Alexander travelled across the desert to consult the oracle. Ancient sources say he was recognised as the son of Zeus-Ammon, the Greek-Egyptian form of Amun. This was not merely a spiritual moment. It gave Alexander religious and political legitimacy as ruler of Egypt and allowed him to present himself in pharaonic terms.

The surviving temple is worn but powerful.

Its position above Aghurmi, the remains of reliefs, and the memory of its ancient authority make it the most historically significant site in Siwa.

## Cleopatra's Spring

Cleopatra's Spring is one of Siwa's best-known gathering places.

The circular stone pool is fed by natural spring water at a constant 29 to 30°C and has been used for generations. Cafes and juice stands cluster nearby, and visitors often combine it with the Oracle, which lies close by.

The name is more romantic than certain.

There is no firm evidence that Cleopatra VII bathed here. The spring is genuinely ancient, known in classical antiquity, and may correspond to what Herodotus called the "Fountain of the Sun," but the queen's name belongs more to later storytelling and tourism than documented history.

That does not make the place less worth visiting.

It only means you should enjoy the water without needing the legend to do all the work.

## Mountain of the Dead

Gebel al-Mawta, the Mountain of the Dead, is a limestone hill covered with rock-cut tombs.

The tombs date from the 26th Dynasty through the Roman period, when Siwa was part of broader Egyptian, Greek, and Roman religious worlds. Several retain painted decoration, including the Tomb of Si-Amun, known for its vivid Ptolemaic-period scenes and vine-and-grape ceiling.

During the Second World War, local people used some of these tombs as shelters during Italian bombing raids.

That layering is typical of Siwa.

Ancient burial chambers become wartime refuge. Sacred landscapes become everyday geography.

## The Great Sand Sea

South of the oasis lies the Great Sand Sea.

This is one of the largest dune fields on earth — approximately 72,000 square kilometres of erg stretching west into Libya — with immense waves of sand, ridges, bowls, and horizons that shift with the light. Desert safaris from Siwa usually head here by 4x4 with local drivers.

Typical excursions include dune driving, sandboarding, sunset, desert tea, and sometimes overnight camping.

Bir Wahed, a hot sulphurous spring near the edge of the dunes, is one of the classic stops. Soaking in warm water in the desert after sunset is one of Siwa's strongest experiences when done responsibly.

Do not enter the Great Sand Sea independently.

Use experienced local drivers and follow current security and permit guidance.

## Salt Lakes and Fatnas Island

Siwa's salt lakes have become some of the oasis's most photographed places.

Their hypersaline water makes floating effortless, and the lakes can appear turquoise, white, mirror-like, or surreal depending on light, salt crust, and season. The lakes are visually beautiful but also fragile and industrial in places, shaped by salt extraction as much as natural scenery.

Fatnas Island, also known as Fantasy Island, sits on Birket Siwa and is a classic sunset point.

Palms, still water, and low desert light make it one of the easiest places to understand why Siwa captures people.

Remember what the water is for.

Siwa exists because of it. Freshwater springs feed the palms, olives, and gardens, while the salt lakes are bound up with drainage, groundwater, water levels, and salt damage to the land around them. What looks like scenery to a visitor is, for the people who farm around it, a resource and a problem to manage.

Lake by lake detail is in [The Salt Lakes of Siwa](/guide/siwa-oasis/salt-lakes) and [Fatnas Island](/guide/siwa-oasis/fatnas-island). For what floating actually feels like, what to bring, and how to rinse off, read our [guide to the Siwa salt lakes](/blog/siwa-salt-lakes).

## Siwan Culture

Siwa is culturally distinct from most of Egypt.

Many residents speak Siwi, a Berber language UNESCO classifies as definitely endangered. Traditional crafts include embroidery, basketry, pottery, and silver jewellery. Siwan dress, especially bridal clothing and women's jewellery, has its own visual language of black fabric, dense colour, geometric pattern, amber, coral, and silver.

The Siwa House Museum, opened in 1990 with Canadian aid funding, is the best small introduction to this material culture.

It is not large, but it helps visitors see the oasis as a living community rather than only a landscape of ruins and springs.

Respect matters here.

Siwa is conservative, and its social codes around dress, photography, gender, and public behaviour are more reserved than in Cairo, Alexandria, or Red Sea resorts.

In practice, that means covering up in town. If you are going into a spring or a salt lake, bring something to wear over swimwear for the ride there and back. Ask before photographing anyone, and always before photographing women or children.

Not everything in the market is old Siwan craft. Much is made for visitors, and quality varies. That is not a problem in itself, but see the [Siwa House Museum](/guide/siwa-oasis/siwa-house-museum) first, ask about what you are looking at, and then decide.

The same care applies to celebrations. The Siyaha festival in autumn is a gathering of reconciliation and community, not a show staged for visitors, and weddings and religious occasions are not photo opportunities either. If your dates coincide, follow local lead on where to stand and what to photograph. [Events in Siwa](/guide/siwa-oasis/upcoming-events-in-siwa-oasis) has the detail.

## When to Go

October to April is the practical window.

Days are comfortable for walking, cycling, climbing Shali, and going out into the desert. Winter nights are cold, though. Do not assume an oasis is warm after dark, and pack a real warm layer.

Summer is very hot. Sightseeing shrinks to early morning and evening, and it is not a kind season for a first visit, for travellers unused to heat, or for families with children or older relatives.

Ramadan changes the town's rhythm. Days are quieter, evenings livelier, and restaurant and shop hours shift. Travel works, but leave slack for meals and transport.

Month-by-month detail is in [Siwa Weather and Best Time to Visit](/guide/siwa-oasis/weather-in-siwa-oasis).

## How Long to Stay

Two nights is the minimum.

That gives one full day for Shali, the Oracle, Cleopatra's Spring, the Mountain of the Dead, and a sunset at Fatnas, with little room to breathe.

Three nights is better.

It allows a full desert safari, more time in the palm groves, slower mornings, and less pressure on the long arrival and departure days.

Four nights or more suit travellers who want to rest, write, photograph, cycle, visit springs, explore craft traditions, or simply let the oasis stop feeling like a list.

Siwa is not a place to rush.

The whole point is that it is far away.

## Who Siwa Suits

Siwa is not a required stop on a first, short trip to Egypt.

If you have not yet seen [Cairo](/guide/cairo), [Giza](/guide/giza), [Luxor](/guide/luxor), and [Aswan](/guide/aswan), start with the Nile Valley. Siwa costs days of travel, and those days are better spent there first.

It suits travellers who can give it time, who want an Egypt that is not only pyramids and temples, and who care about desert life, language, and the way a community lives around water.

It suits less well anyone who wants quick transfers, a long list of famous sites in a few days, or city-style comfort. They will find it hard work.

Siwa is not an easy place. That is part of its value.

## Final Word

Siwa is one of Egypt's great distances.

Not only in kilometres.

It is distant in language, architecture, rhythm, memory, and landscape. It belongs to the Western Desert, to Berber North Africa, to the cult of Amun, to Alexander's ambition, to salt and palms, to springs and dunes, to a community that has spent centuries negotiating isolation and change.

Come for the oracle, the lakes, or the sand.

Stay long enough to feel the oasis itself.

Siwa is not Egypt's edge because it is empty.

It is Egypt's edge because it is full of a different world.
