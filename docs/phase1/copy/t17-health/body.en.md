## Health and Safety in Egypt

Egypt's health and safety reputation runs significantly worse than the country's actual lived reality for most foreign visitors. The cumulative warnings of older guidebooks, government travel advisories that flatten regional differences, and news cycles focused on rare incidents combine to produce a picture more alarming than what most travellers experience. The two principal risks for foreign visitors are road traffic accidents (more common than any criminal incident in tourist destinations) and gastrointestinal illness (managed by the bottled-water rule covered in the water-safety-in-egypt guide). Personal-crime levels for tourists in Cairo, Alexandria, Luxor, Aswan, and the Red Sea cities are low by comparison with most large urban destinations elsewhere in the world. The genuinely restricted zones — northern Sinai, the deeper Western Desert, certain border areas — are geographically specific and well-documented.

This is a working guide to what to actually prepare for, where the real precautions matter, and how to handle health and safety issues if they arise.

## What the Landscape Actually Looks Like

Egypt operates under generally favourable conditions for foreign tourism. The tourism economy is a significant share of the country's GDP, and the Egyptian government's tourist police presence, security infrastructure at major sites, and broader regulatory framework are all designed to support international visitor flows. The Tourist and Antiquities Police are stationed at the major archaeological sites, the airports, and the heritage districts of the major cities; their visible presence at the entrances of Karnak, the Pyramids, the Egyptian Museum, and similar venues is part of the standard tourism infrastructure.

For health, the country has a tiered medical system. The top-tier private hospitals in Cairo, Alexandria, and the Red Sea cities — covered in the travel-insurance guide — operate at international standards and handle foreign visitors regularly. Pharmacies are widespread and inexpensive. The major Egyptian pharmacy chains (El Ezaby, Roshdi, Misr Pharmacies) operate across the country, with 24-hour locations in Cairo, Alexandria, and the major tourist cities. Most medications that require prescriptions in Western countries are available over the counter in Egypt, which simplifies the routine "minor ailment" question substantially.

For safety, the country has been generally safe for international tourists since the security stabilisation following the 2011 revolution and the subsequent 2013-2017 period. The North Sinai counter-insurgency operations against ISIS-affiliated militant groups have been geographically contained to the north of the Sinai peninsula and have not directly affected the Sinai resort areas (Sharm El Sheikh, Dahab, Saint Catherine's, Taba); the broader tourism corridors are operationally normal. The Egyptian Tourist Police number is 126 for non-emergency tourism-related contacts; the general police number is 122; the ambulance number is 123; the fire-and-rescue number is 180.

The deeper context — Egypt's overall security architecture, the country's regulatory framework around tourism, the broader geopolitical considerations — sits outside the scope of a practical traveller guide. The standard pragmatic position is that Egypt is safer for foreign visitors than the country's reputation often suggests, while still requiring the geographic awareness covered later in this piece.

## Pre-Trip Health Preparation

Most travellers to Egypt do not need extensive health preparation, but a small number of considerations are worth handling before departure.

Routine vaccinations should be up to date — tetanus boosters, polio, measles-mumps-rubella (MMR), varicella, influenza. Egypt does not require these for entry; the recommendations come from general international travel health guidance.

Hepatitis A vaccination is widely recommended for travel to Egypt and most of the Middle East. The standard two-dose course provides long-term protection; a single dose at least two weeks before departure offers substantial protection for short trips. Hepatitis B vaccination is recommended for longer stays, work-related travel, or any traveller with planned medical-procedure exposure. Typhoid vaccination is recommended for travellers planning off-the-beaten-path itineraries, rural Egypt visits, or extended stays. The standard oral or injectable typhoid vaccines provide reasonable protection.

Rabies pre-exposure vaccination is sometimes recommended for travellers planning extended stays, animal-contact work, or rural travel. Egypt has been endemic for rabies historically (cluster-consistent with the traveling-with-kids guide's caution about stray cats and dogs). Pre-exposure vaccination simplifies post-exposure treatment if a bite incident occurs but does not eliminate the need for medical evaluation. The standard alternative is to seek post-exposure prophylaxis immediately if bitten.

Yellow Fever vaccination is required only for travellers entering Egypt from a yellow-fever-endemic country (most of sub-Saharan Africa and some South American countries). Travellers transiting through yellow-fever-endemic airports may need to present a vaccination certificate at Egyptian immigration. The yellow fever vaccine is administered as a single dose providing lifetime protection.

COVID-19 is not currently required for entry as of 2026; the Egyptian government dropped COVID-related entry requirements in mid-2023 and they have not been reinstated. Routine vaccination is recommended for general travel health.

Routine prescription medication should be carried in original containers with prescription documentation. Egyptian customs occasionally examine medication imports; a documented prescription resolves most questions. Bring enough supply for the trip plus a buffer; major Egyptian pharmacies stock most international and regional generic equivalents but specific brand availability varies.

A small first-aid kit is worth bringing: paracetamol/acetaminophen, ibuprofen, oral rehydration salts (or buy locally — the Rehydran brand at any Egyptian pharmacy), antihistamine for stings and rashes, antiseptic wipes, plasters, motion sickness medication if needed.

Travel insurance is essential rather than optional. The travel-insurance guide covers the comprehensive framework — including the specific clauses (medical evacuation, adventure-activity coverage, pre-existing condition handling) that matter for Egypt specifically.

## Common Health Issues on the Ground

Most foreign visitors to Egypt experience some minor health issue during a trip. The most common issues are predictable and manageable.

Traveller's diarrhoea is the most frequent health complaint. Usually traced to a specific meal or beverage exposure, typically a 24-48 hour episode handled with clear fluids, oral rehydration salts (Rehydran or equivalent), rest, and gradual return to plain food. The water-safety-in-egypt guide covers prevention; the tips-for-families and traveling-with-kids guides cover child-specific treatment. Persistent diarrhoea longer than 48 hours, high fever, blood in stool, or signs of dehydration warrant a doctor visit.

Dehydration and heat-related illness is the second most common health concern, particularly for visitors travelling in summer or visiting Upper Egypt in shoulder seasons. The egypt-weather-guide covers the underlying climate framework. Practical signs of dehydration: dark urine, headache, fatigue, irritability. Practical signs of heat exhaustion: flushed skin, rapid pulse, nausea, confusion. The standard response is move to shade or air conditioning, hydrate with cool water and ORS, rest. Severe heat exhaustion or any signs of heat stroke (very high body temperature, altered consciousness, lack of sweating) warrants immediate medical attention.

Sun exposure and sunburn affect almost all visitors in some form. High-SPF mineral sunscreen (SPF 30 minimum, SPF 50 better) applied liberally and reapplied every two hours outdoors is the operational guidance. Hats, long-sleeve protective clothing, and UV-blocking sunglasses all reduce cumulative exposure. The egypt-weather-guide and tips-for-families guides cover the clothing implications.

Cairo air pollution is a real concern for travellers with respiratory conditions. Cairo regularly ranks among the world's most air-polluted megacities, particularly in winter when temperature inversions trap pollution near ground level. PM2.5 levels exceed WHO guidelines for substantial portions of the year. For travellers with asthma, COPD, or other respiratory sensitivities, bring inhalers and consider N95 masks during high-pollution periods. The Upper Egyptian cities (Luxor, Aswan) have significantly cleaner air than Cairo; the Red Sea coast cleaner still. Cairo air quality monitors are published in real time by the Egyptian Environmental Affairs Agency.

Mosquito-borne illness is generally minimal in Egypt. Malaria has been effectively eliminated from most of the country, with the Fayoum oasis area historically the only intermittent concern during summer months. West Nile virus has been reported rarely in the Nile Valley and Delta. Standard mosquito-bite prevention — repellent containing DEET or picaridin, long-sleeve clothing in mosquito-active hours, screens or nets at accommodation in lower-tier rural areas — covers the practical risk.

Bilharzia (schistosomiasis) is covered in the water-safety-in-egypt guide. The single most important practical health precaution: do not swim or wade in the Nile or its irrigation canals.

Animal-bite considerations. Egypt has substantial feral cat populations and a smaller feral dog population. Rabies is not eliminated. The standard advice — covered in traveling-with-kids — is to avoid touching stray animals and to seek immediate medical evaluation after any bite or scratch that breaks the skin. Post-exposure rabies prophylaxis is available at major Egyptian hospitals; immediate cleaning of the wound with soap and water followed by professional medical evaluation is the standard protocol.

Food-borne illness beyond traveller's diarrhoea is uncommon at hotels and major restaurants but occurs occasionally at street-food venues. The tips-for-families and culinary-journey-in-egypt guides cover food-safety framing.

## Medical Care if You Need It

The Egyptian medical care system is tiered. The top-tier private hospitals in Cairo (Cleopatra Hospital Group, Dar Al Fouad, Saudi German Hospital Cairo, As-Salam International), Alexandria (Saudi German Hospital Alexandria, Smouha International), Luxor (International Hospital Luxor), Aswan, Hurghada (Hurghada International Hospital, Red Sea Hospital), and Sharm El Sheikh (Sharm International Hospital) — all named in the travel-insurance guide — operate at international standards. English-speaking staff, modern equipment, accepting international insurance directly in many cases.

For routine non-urgent care, private clinics in Cairo, Alexandria, and the major tourism cities offer consultations at modest cost. The equivalent of 30-60 US dollars for a standard doctor consultation; specialised services and tests at proportionally higher rates. Many private clinics accept walk-in patients without appointments during business hours.

For prescription pharmaceuticals, the major Egyptian pharmacy chains (El Ezaby, Roshdi, Misr Pharmacies) stock a wide range of medications. Many medications that require prescriptions in Western countries are available over the counter or with a quick pharmacist consultation in Egypt. The pharmacist consultation is free; medications are typically inexpensive — common items run the equivalent of a few US dollars.

For emergency medical care, the major private hospital emergency rooms are the standard destination for serious incidents. Direct billing to international insurance is increasingly common at the top-tier facilities; less common at smaller facilities, where upfront payment with insurance reimbursement is the practical fallback. The travel-insurance guide covers the operational specifics.

For the bilharzia question specifically, treatment is straightforward with the antiparasitic medication praziquantel; cluster-consistent with the water-safety-in-egypt guide.

For diving-related medical emergencies (decompression sickness, the hyperbaric chamber question), the dedicated hyperbaric chambers at Sharm El Sheikh and Dahab handle the Red Sea region. The travel-insurance guide covers the DAN (Divers Alert Network) insurance framework.

For mental health emergencies, the major private hospital networks in Cairo have psychiatric and psychological services; access varies by facility. Crisis hotlines in Arabic exist but English-language services are limited.

## Personal Safety: The Honest Picture

Personal safety for tourists in Egypt's major destinations is broadly comparable to other major Mediterranean and Middle Eastern travel destinations. The honest picture, with appropriate calibration:

Petty crime — pickpocketing, occasional bag-snatching, scams at major tourist sites — does occur, particularly in dense market areas (Khan el-Khalili, the area around the Egyptian Museum, the Pyramids approach). The defensive measures are standard: avoid carrying valuables in back pockets, use front-facing bags in markets, keep travel documents in hotel safes when not needed, don't display expensive cameras and jewellery prominently. Most pickpocketing incidents are non-violent and recoverable through normal hotel-front-desk and tourist-police procedures.

Violent crime against tourists is uncommon in the major destinations. Random violent attacks on foreign visitors at tourist sites are statistically rare. The most common reported safety incidents are commercial disputes (taxi overcharging, shop tout escalation, the predatory-bakshish framings covered in the touts-in-egypt and tipping-in-egypt guides) rather than criminal incidents.

Demonstrations and political activity are uncommon in Cairo and other major cities since the 2013 stabilisation period. Occasional smaller demonstrations occur; the standard advice is to avoid them and to move calmly to a different area if encountered. The major heritage districts and tourist areas are not typical demonstration locations.

Road traffic accidents are the largest single source of foreign-visitor injury in Egypt. The transportation-in-egypt guide covers the framework — Cairo driving is intense, intercity highways are well-maintained but variably enforced, the standard advice is to hire a car with a driver rather than self-drive, and night-driving on rural roads is genuinely difficult. For pedestrians, crossing Cairo streets requires deliberate confidence and patience; using crosswalks where available, maintaining steady pace once committed, and using the pedestrian flow of Egyptian crowds as cover are the practical techniques.

Drug laws are strict. Cannabis, prescription medications used without prescription, and any controlled substance are subject to substantial penalties under Egyptian law. The standard practical position: do not use, carry, or be associated with illegal substances during travel in Egypt. Possession charges can result in significant prison sentences and complex consular intervention.

Photography restrictions matter operationally. Military installations, government buildings, bridges, transit infrastructure, and some border areas are off-limits for photography. Camera confiscation is a real risk for non-compliance. The cultural-etiquette-in-egypt guide covers the broader photography framework.

Embassy registration through the home country's foreign service (US STEP, UK FCDO equivalent) is sensible for longer stays and provides emergency contact in case of significant incidents. Most embassies are clustered in central Cairo's Garden City district; verify location and hours before departure.

## Restricted Areas and Travel Advisories

The geographical distinction between safe and restricted areas in Egypt matters operationally.

Northern Sinai — the area north of the Gulf of Suez line, including Al-Arish, Rafah area, and the northeastern Sinai interior — has been under continuous Egyptian government security operations since 2013 against ISIS-affiliated militant groups. Foreign tourist travel into northern Sinai is not permitted in most circumstances; the entry points are controlled. The major government travel advisories (US State Department, UK FCDO, Canada Global Affairs, Australia DFAT, Germany Auswärtiges Amt, France France Diplomatie) generally advise against all travel to northern Sinai.

Southern Sinai resort areas — Sharm El Sheikh, Dahab, Nuweiba, Taba, Saint Catherine's, the southern Sinai Mountains — operate normally for tourism. The travel advisories distinguish between these and northern Sinai. Most advisories rate the southern Sinai resort areas at the same general-precaution level as the rest of Egypt.

The Western Desert along the Libyan border has restricted access in some areas. Bahariya, Farafra, Dakhla, Kharga, and Siwa oases are operationally accessible to foreign visitors, though deep desert travel and travel toward the immediate Libyan border may require permits or local guide accompaniment.

The Sudanese border area in the southern Egyptian desert has restricted access.

The Halayeb Triangle and Sudan border zones are administratively disputed and have restricted access.

Most major travel advisories provide their specific guidance through dedicated country pages updated regularly:

- UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO): gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/egypt<sp>
- Canada Global Affairs: travel.gc.ca<sp>
- Australia Smartraveller: smartraveller.gov.au<sp>
- Germany Auswärtiges Amt: auswaertiges-amt.de<sp>
- France France Diplomatie: diplomatie.gouv.fr<sp>

These advisories are honest framework references for travellers planning a trip; their general advice for the main Egyptian tourist destinations (Cairo, Luxor, Aswan, the southern Sinai resorts, the Red Sea coast, the major Western Desert oases) has been at the "exercise normal precautions" or "exercise increased caution" levels for several years, with the specific exceptions noted above.

A practical note. Travel insurance policies generally cover only travel to destinations not subject to a travel advisory's highest warning level. Verify your policy's geographic coverage if any part of an itinerary touches a restricted area.

## Final Word

Egypt's health and safety landscape is more navigable than the cumulative warnings of older guidebooks suggest. The principal risks for most foreign visitors are road traffic accidents and gastrointestinal illness, both manageable with standard precautions. The serious security restrictions — northern Sinai, the deeper Western Desert, certain border zones — are geographically specific and well-documented. The standard Egyptian itinerary is safer than the country's reputation implies. The smartest precautions are also the simplest.
