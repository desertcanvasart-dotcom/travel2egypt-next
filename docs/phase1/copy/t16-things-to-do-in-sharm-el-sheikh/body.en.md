[Sharm el-Sheikh](/guide/sharm-el-sheikh) is usually described as a beach resort, and it is one.

Hotels, pools, seaside restaurants, and all-inclusive holidays are a large part of the town. Stopping there wastes it a little.

Sharm sits at the southern tip of the Sinai Peninsula, where the Gulf of Aqaba meets the Red Sea. Offshore are reefs with an international reputation; behind the town are the mountains and desert of South Sinai. Further inland lie Mount Sinai and the St Catherine area.

The way to use Sharm well is as a resort that also takes the sea and the desert seriously.

## See the sea at Ras Mohammed

The strongest reason to come to Sharm el-Sheikh is the Red Sea underwater world, and its centre is [Ras Mohammed National Park](/guide/sharm-el-sheikh/ras-mohammed-national-park) at the southern tip of the peninsula.

Ras Mohammed is known as Egypt's first designated national park. It protects sea and land together: coral reefs, mangroves, desert landforms, and the habitats of seabirds and fish. As a day trip from Sharm, it shows a density of sea life that the resort beaches do not.

For divers, Shark Reef and Yolanda Reef are the well-known names. In places the currents bring pelagic fish past rich coral walls. Certified divers who choose sites to match their experience get Red Sea visibility and vertical drop-offs.

Non-divers have plenty here too. Snorkelling from a boat, shallow-water viewing, and tours that include the land side of the park suit different ages and levels of stamina. If you are not a confident swimmer, stay close to the boat, wear a life vest, and keep within the guide's range rather than heading far out.

On the reef, the basics are simple: don't touch, don't stand, don't chase. Red Sea coral is still comparatively healthy in places, but it is under pressure from tourism and rising water temperatures. Sunscreen, the way you use fins, and how you enter from the boat all leave a mark.

Ras Mohammed is not only for photographs. It is the clearest way to understand why Sharm grew up here.

## Strait of Tiran and boat trips

North-east of Sharm is the Strait of Tiran, with reefs such as Jackson, Woodhouse, Thomas, and Gordon that are popular with experienced divers.

Currents can be strong, and these are not always sites for beginners. The underwater landscape is a large part of the appeal, but whether to dive them depends on your certification, number of dives, and the day's conditions. A good dive centre chooses the site for safety, not only because a customer asks for it.

Snorkelling trips are easier or harder depending on the sea. With small children or weaker swimmers, check the boat's facilities, the number of guides, life vests, lunch, and shade before you book.

In Sharm, the cheapest boat trip is not always the better deal. Equipment condition, the quality of the briefing, safety management, and group size show up directly in the day at sea.

## [Naama Bay](/guide/sharm-el-sheikh/naama-bay-sharm-el-sheikh-guide) and the beaches

Naama Bay grew up as Sharm el-Sheikh's tourist centre. Hotels, restaurants, cafés, dive centres, and a seafront promenade are all close together, which makes it an easy base for a first stay.

The beach is well equipped, with food and rest close at hand, so it suits families and first Red Sea trips. If you are after quiet nature, it can feel commercial. Early morning is the calmest time, when both the sea and the town are easier to see.

Shark's Bay, Ras Um Sid, and the area around El Fanar have snorkelling from the shore. Hotels and beach clubs often control access; if you want to use a beach other than your own hotel's, check the day-use terms in advance.

The coastline is not open in the same way everywhere. Resorts, public beaches, beach clubs, jetties, and protected coral areas are mixed together. When choosing where to stay, ask how you get into the sea, not only how big the room is.

Beyond snorkelling and diving, kitesurfing, waterskiing, and windsurfing are offered along the coast. For anyone who would rather stay dry, glass-bottom boat trips show the reef from above.

## Going into the desert

Sharm is not only the sea. Behind the coast are the dry mountains and wadis of South Sinai, and heading inland at sunset changes the impression of the resort considerably.

Desert tours offer quad bikes, jeeps, camel rides, and stargazing. Quality varies. Some are a short stop for photographs; others, with Bedouin guides, show you the landforms, water sources, plants, and the knowledge of people who live here.

Stargazing evenings often begin with dinner in a Bedouin-style camp, followed by time at telescopes under a dark sky, with the Milky Way visible when conditions allow.

The Bedouin communities of South Sinai know this land as a place to live, not as a backdrop. Good tours respect that knowledge. What matters is whether a tour only stages something desert-like or actually teaches you to read the place.

Evening into night suits the desert best: you avoid the daytime heat and see the colour change on the rock and then the stars. Even in winter, nights turn cold, so carry a light layer.

## Mount Sinai and St Catherine

Several hours inland from Sharm are Mount Sinai and [St Catherine](/guide/saint-catherine), where religious, historical, and landscape meanings overlap. It is one of the most important destinations in southern Sinai.

Mount Sinai has been associated with Moses in Jewish, Christian, and Islamic tradition. Many travellers climb at night, watch the sunrise from the summit, and walk down. It is less a technical climb than a long mountain walk, done in the dark, at altitude, and cold in winter. Walking shoes, warm clothing, water, and a torch are essential.

St Catherine's Monastery was built in the 6th century under Justinian I and is one of the oldest monasteries in the world. It is a working religious community, not simply a visitor attraction. Visiting hours, dress, photography, and which areas are open are all restricted.

Access to this area can change with local regulations, the monastery's own arrangements, religious events, and security decisions. If you plan to go during a stay in Sharm, check with a reliable operator and official sources both before you travel and after you arrive. ## Old Sharm and Naama Bay

Sharm el-Sheikh does not have a single centre. Naama Bay was built for tourism and is easy to walk, with food and tour bookings close at hand.

Around Sharm el-Maya, the old market area has more of a local feel: the harbour, markets, mosques, and everyday shops, a side of the town you do not see from a resort.

Al-Mustafa Mosque has a striking exterior and is sometimes lit at night. It is a place of worship, so dress accordingly and take care with photographs. There is also a Coptic Orthodox church, a reminder that Sharm is an Egyptian town with residents' religious life, not only a town for foreign visitors.

Stay in Naama Bay, eat and walk in the old market, and head out to the sea and the desert. Combined that way, Sharm becomes more than its resort surface.

## Choosing a dive centre

Sharm has many dive centres. With so much choice, don't decide on price alone.

Check that courses and guiding follow a recognised certification agency, that equipment is maintained, that your experience is properly checked, that the safety briefing is thorough, and that the ratio of divers to guides is not too high.

For a first dive, what matters is shallow water, a clear explanation, and nobody pushing you. If you swim weakly, are diving with children, or are returning after a long break, say so at the start. A good operator will not treat it as a nuisance.

For certified divers, Red Sea visibility can make conditions look easier than they are. At parts of Tiran and Ras Mohammed, the day's conditions change the difficulty. There is no need to push to sites beyond your experience.

## When to go

Sharm el-Sheikh works all year, but different activities suit different months.

To enjoy both the sea and the desert, October to April works well. Gentler temperatures make daytime movement and desert trips easier. European winter holidays bring crowds, so book hotels and popular trips early.

Summer is good for being in the water, but the heat on land is strong. Desert tours, walks in the old town, and trips towards Mount Sinai need careful timing and stamina.

For diving and snorkelling, wind, waves, tide, and visibility matter as much as the season. Don't pin a sea trip to a single day; leave room to choose a good one.

## Safety and travel advice

Sharm el-Sheikh receives many travellers as one of Egypt's main Red Sea resorts. Conditions in Sinai vary greatly by area, so don't treat the South Sinai resorts and North Sinai as the same place.

Before you travel, check your own government's travel advice. Official advice, such as that of the US State Department, carries general cautions for Egypt and stronger warnings for North Sinai and some other areas. [VERIFY: current official travel advice for Sharm el-Sheikh, South Sinai and North Sinai] For resort stays, the national park, and inland tours, follow local regulations and your operator's judgement.

At sea, safety management decides the quality of the day. Don't dive when unwell, don't swim after drinking alcohol, don't stand on coral, and keep to the boat's meeting times. Basic care removes most of the risk.

In the desert, don't go alone, carry water, and prepare for the cold at night. For the mountain or the monastery, road restrictions, permits, and the need for a local guide can change; don't set off on your own judgement.

## The best use of Sharm

Sharm el-Sheikh works for a do-nothing holiday too, but its real strength is moving between rest and adventure.

A morning at sea. An afternoon at the hotel. An evening in the old market or Naama Bay. Another day in the desert. With the energy for it, a day to Mount Sinai or St Catherine.

That pace suits Sharm.

Seen only as a resort, Sharm can look like any number of seaside destinations. With Ras Mohammed's underwater walls, the rock peaks of southern Sinai, Bedouin knowledge of the land, and the weight of religious history taken together, it becomes a far deeper place.
