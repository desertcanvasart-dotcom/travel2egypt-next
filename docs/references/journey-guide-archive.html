Egypt Travel Guide

Operator-grade guides to Egypt's cities, by the people who run trips here every week.

Egypt is bigger than its postcard.

Most first-time visitors arrive with a short list — Giza, a Nile cruise, a few days at the Red Sea — and that itinerary works. It also leaves the country reduced to a sequence of monuments rather than a place with cities, coasts, deserts and a present-day rhythm five thousand years deep.

This is a working reference to more than forty Egyptian cities, written by people who actually operate trips here and organised the way Egyptians think about their own country: by region, by what each place is genuinely for, and by the kind of traveller it suits. Not a brochure. Use it to plan, to skip the parts everyone recommends but you won't enjoy, and to find the parts almost no one mentions.

First trip?

Start with Cairo, Luxor and Aswan — the pharaonic heart runs through these three. Everything below is for return visits, longer trips, and specific interests.

Cairo & Giza

The capital and its inseparable twin, where the Pyramids sit on what used to be desert and is now a city edge. Where most trips begin — and where most underestimate the time.

Essential

Cairo

The start of nearly every trip and the city most travellers shortchange. The Egyptian Museum, Islamic and Coptic Cairo, the markets — three days, minimum, before you've really seen it.
Essential

Giza

The Pyramids and the Sphinx, on the desert edge the city has grown up to meet.
Worth a detour

Saqqara & Memphis

The step pyramid and the older capital — quieter, and where the story actually starts.
Skip unless

The Delta towns

Tanta, Zagazig and the agricultural heart — for specific historical interest, not a first trip.

Upper Egypt

Luxor and Aswan and the temples strung between them. This is the Nile-cruise corridor and the part of Egypt most visitors actually come for — the densest pharaonic landscape in the world.

Essential

Luxor

The greatest open-air museum there is: Karnak and the temple of Luxor on the east bank, the Valley of the Kings and Hatshepsut on the west. Two full days, and you've only scratched it.
Essential

Aswan

Egypt's gentlest city, where the Nile changes character and Nubia begins.
Exceptional

Abu Simbel

Ramses' colossi, moved stone by stone above the lake — a day-flight south of Aswan.
Worth it

Dendera (Qena)

The best-preserved temple ceiling in Egypt, and far quieter than Luxor.
On the cruise

Edfu & Kom Ombo

The river temples the cruises moor for, between the two cities.
Worth it

Esna

A small, vividly painted temple below the modern street — and the cruise lock.

The Delta & North Coast

A different Egypt — Greek, Roman and Ottoman layers, cooler air, the Mediterranean. The country's second city and the coast running east and west of it.

Exceptional

Alexandria

Not pharaonic and not trying to be — a faded Mediterranean capital of libraries, corniche cafés and Greco-Roman remains. The antidote to a trip spent entirely among temples.
Worth it

Rosetta (Rashid)

Where the Nile meets the sea and Ottoman merchant houses still stand — the stone's hometown.
Skip unless

Marsa Matruh

Summer beach for domestic travellers — skip unless that's your season.
Skip unless

The Canal cities

Port Said, Ismailia, Suez — for maritime or modern-history interest only.

The Red Sea Coast

Egypt's beach-and-reef country — where most trips come down off the history. The reefs that made it one of the world's best-known dive destinations, and a string of resorts from polished to wild.

For the right traveller

Hurghada

The Red Sea's workhorse — the most reefs, the easiest logistics, the shortest transfer. Not pretty, but it works, and the diving is the point.
For the right traveller

El Gouna

A built-from-scratch lagoon town; the Red Sea at its most polished.
For divers

Marsa Alam

The wilder south — the reefs people fly past Hurghada for.
Quieter

Safaga

Low-key, kite-able, and the port for the deep southern dive sites.

Sinai

The peninsula between Egypt and the wider Middle East, in two distinct halves: the coastal dive resorts, and the high granite interior around the Monastery of St Catherine.

For the right traveller

Sharm El Sheikh

Sinai's dive capital and the easiest base for the reefs of Ras Mohammed and Tiran — and the launch point for the desert interior behind it.
For divers

Dahab

Where Sinai slows down — a backpacker-grown dive town, not a resort.
For pilgrims & trekkers

St Catherine

The world's oldest working monastery, at the foot of the mountain you climb before dawn.
Quieter still

Nuweiba & Taba

The far coast toward the border — empty beaches, little else.

The Western Desert

The chain of inhabited oases west of the Nile — the least-visited part of Egypt, and arguably the most rewarding for travellers who can spare the days for sand seas and silence.

For sand & silence

Bahariya & the White Desert

The closest oasis to Cairo and the gateway to the white chalk formations of the open desert — the classic overnight under the stars.
Deep cut

Dakhla

Mudbrick villages and hot springs, deep in the sand sea.
Deep cut

Farafra

The slowest oasis pace, on the edge of the White Desert.
Deep cut

Kharga

The southern oasis, with Roman temples almost no traveller reaches.

Middle Egypt

The Nile Valley between Cairo and Luxor, mostly skipped by international itineraries. For serious Egyptophiles willing to spend a few extra days on a valley tourism hasn't reshaped.

For Egyptophiles

Minya

The base for Beni Hasan and Tell el-Amarna — Akhenaten's lost capital, at your own pace.
For Egyptophiles

Abydos (Sohag)

Seti I's temple and the holiest ground of the Osiris cult.
Waypoint

Asyut

A practical stop on the long valley road — a waypoint, not a destination.
How this guide works

What this guide won't do.

It won't call every city magical. Some are practical waypoints, and the guide says so.
It won't sell "hidden gems" vaguely. Hidden from whom? A site quiet in international tourism may be packed with Egyptians. We're explicit about which.
It won't pad with filler. Every city page tells you what it is, what's worth your time, how long to stay, and how to get there — in operator's voice, not the tourism-board version.
It won't push paid placements. The recommendations are where we actually book travellers and where we send ourselves.

Egypt does not fit into a week. It does not really fit into a month.

Pick your part of it — a focused first visit, or the desert, the Mediterranean, the Upper Egypt the cruises skip on a later return.

The rest is here when you come back.
