The river, honestly

Everyone wants a Nile cruise. Not everyone wants the same one.

What the cruise really is, who it suits, and who should think twice — before the boats and the itineraries below.

A Nile cruise is the oldest way to see Egypt and still the best: you sleep in one bed for the whole journey while the country slides past the window — palm villages, water buffalo, the temples appearing on the bank exactly where the river left them. Between Luxor and Aswan the great sites stand close to the water, so the sailing does the work of the transfers, and the days fall into an easy rhythm of a morning ashore and a long afternoon watching the banks go by.

It is the part of an Egypt trip people remember most. But "Nile cruise" covers three quite different holidays, and the boat you pick matters more than the stops, because every cruiser visits the same handful of temples. So we'd rather you choose the kind of river you want first.

Who it works for

  • First-time visitors who want the headline temples without daily packing and driving.
  • Couples and families who like a base that moves itself.
  • Anyone who'd take a slow afternoon on deck over another museum.

Who should think twice

  • If you want to study one site in depth, the cruise pace will frustrate you — a day tour from Luxor serves you better.
  • If you're prone to restlessness, four nights on a ship can feel long; the shorter Luxor–Aswan leg suits you.
  • If you want nightlife and crowds, this isn't that — the river is quiet by design.

Orientation

How the river runs.

Every cruise sails this same stretch between Luxor and Aswan. Here's the order of it, and what each stop is for.

Luxor1–2 nights · embark

The world's greatest open-air museum: Karnak and the temple of Luxor on the east bank, the Valley of the Kings and Hatshepsut's terraces on the west. Most cruises begin or end here, with a night or two before sailing.

Esnapassing · the lock

The river narrows to a lock here; ships often queue, and the wait on deck — kids hawking tablecloths thrown up from rowing boats — is part of the lore. A small, well-preserved temple sits in the modern town below street level.

Edfumorning ashore

The temple of Horus — the most completely preserved in Egypt, walls intact to the roofline. Reached by horse-carriage from the mooring, usually before the heat.

Kom Omboafternoon ashore

The double temple on a bend of the river, shared between two gods, with its small museum of mummified crocodiles. Best at dusk, lit, right at the water's edge.

Aswan1–2 nights · the south

Egypt's gentlest city: Philae temple on its island, the High Dam, felucca sailing round Elephantine, and the long day-trip south to Abu Simbel. The river is at its most beautiful here — the turn of most cruises.
