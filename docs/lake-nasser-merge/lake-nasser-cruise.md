# Lake Nasser Cruise: What It Is, and How to Choose One

*Drowned Nubia, rescued temples, Abu Simbel approached from the water — and the practical choices to make before you book: how many nights, which direction, which boat, which season.*

*By Faris Hussein*

---

A Lake Nasser cruise is not an extension of a normal Nile cruise.

Between Luxor and Aswan, the Nile is busy. Towns line the banks, fields run down to the water, boats pass all day, and every temple has its tourist rhythm. Lake Nasser has almost none of that. Water, desert, cliffs, sky, and a handful of relocated temples. That is all.

The quiet is beautiful, but it is not only scenery. Lake Nasser is not a natural lake. It is the reservoir behind the Aswan High Dam, and under it lie the villages, fields, cemeteries and sacred places of Lower Nubia. To cruise it is to travel over modern Egypt's biggest engineering decision, the land and communities it cost, and the ancient temples that were cut up and carried to safety.

That is why choosing a Lake Nasser cruise works differently. Star ratings matter less than trip length, time at Abu Simbel, the quality of the guiding, and how well the cruise fits the rest of your itinerary. On this lake, matching the trip to the character of the place matters more than the boat itself.

## Why the lake exists

The Aswan High Dam was one of the great national projects of twentieth-century Egypt. Its purposes were clear: control the Nile flood, stabilise irrigation, generate electricity, and support a modern state. It did all of that, and it changed Egyptian farming and water management for good.

The cost was also large. As the water rose through the 1960s, a long stretch of Lower Nubia disappeared under the new lake. Around 100,000 Nubians, on both the Egyptian and Sudanese sides, were moved from villages their families had lived in for generations. The resettlement towns gave them new houses. They could not move the old life along the river, the relationship with the land, or the shape of the communities.

Look at Lake Nasser without knowing this and you see a lot of water. Look at it knowing this and the silence means something different.

## The rescue that moved the temples

The dam also set off one of the largest rescues in the history of archaeology.

In the 1960s, an international campaign led by UNESCO recorded and relocated the temples and sites of Nubia before the water reached them. The most famous is Abu Simbel. Ramesses II's Great Temple and the smaller temple of Nefertari were cut into blocks, lifted above the new waterline, and reassembled against an artificial hill. Philae was dismantled and rebuilt on a neighbouring island near Aswan. Kalabsha was moved to a site just south of the High Dam. Smaller temples along the lake were moved in groups to new sites on higher ground.

This matters when you visit. Most of the temples you see on Lake Nasser are not where they were built. They are rescued buildings, in new places and new settings, preserved rather than lost. Seeing them is seeing ancient Egypt and the twentieth-century salvage effort at the same time.

## What you actually see

A Lake Nasser cruise is built around Abu Simbel and the rescued Nubian temples along the lake shore. Most itineraries cover the same core:

**Abu Simbel.** You can reach Abu Simbel by road or by air, but arriving by water changes the experience. Ramesses II built the temple to impress anyone coming up the Nile. Approaching from the lake gives back some of that original effect: the boat moves across open water, and the huge façade appears beyond the desert cliffs. It is not the Abu Simbel you walk up to from a car park.

Twice a year, around 22 February and 22 October, the morning sun reaches the statues in the inner sanctuary of the Great Temple. The alignment survived the relocation. What the dates originally marked is still debated. The light reaching the back of the temple is not.

**Wadi el-Sebua.** A temple of Ramesses II approached by an avenue of sphinxes, which gives the site its name, "Valley of the Lions." It was later used as a church, and Christian paintings sit over and beside the pharaonic reliefs. It shows that a new religion does not always destroy a sacred place. Sometimes it keeps using it and reads it differently.

**Dakka and Maharraqa.** These two smaller temples, one Ptolemaic and one Roman, were moved to the same site as Wadi el-Sebua. Usually you see all three in one stop.

**Amada.** One of the oldest temples in Nubia, begun in the Eighteenth Dynasty. It is small and nothing like Abu Simbel in scale, but the reliefs inside still hold their colour, and up close they are some of the finest on the lake.

**Derr.** A rock-cut temple of Ramesses II, now rebuilt near Amada and usually visited with it.

**Qasr Ibrim.** This one is different. It was never moved. The fortress hill stood high enough to stay above the water and now looks like an island in the lake. Landing is usually not allowed, so you see it from the deck. Even from the boat, this site with its long history of occupation reminds you that Lake Nasser is more than temple-hopping.

If you only want Abu Simbel, a day trip from Aswan will get you there. The reason to choose the cruise is the rest: seeing these lakeside temples quietly, in a setting most itineraries never reach. Lake Nasser is not just a convenient way to Abu Simbel. It is a route along the edge of drowned Nubia, with Abu Simbel included.

## How it differs from a Luxor–Aswan cruise

There are fewer boats, few town stops, and much more time on board. This is not a trip for watching life on the riverbank. It is a trip between wide water and desert. Nights are dark, the sky is huge, and there is very little sound.

That also means limited free time ashore. There is no town to step into, no shops to browse, no evening market. The boat becomes the place you live: reading on deck, watching the water, the evening light, the stars, the guide's talks before each temple.

Some people find this boring. For people who value it, Lake Nasser fits better than almost anything else in Egypt. A Luxor–Aswan cruise combines temples, towns, river life and the rhythm of mainstream tourism. Lake Nasser is made of fewer things: water, desert, rescued temples, and the memory of Nubia. Having so few elements is the point of the trip.

## How many nights

Standard Lake Nasser cruises run three or four nights.

**Three nights** is efficient but tight. It is less about seeing each temple calmly and more about short connections between the main stops. It is realistic if you are short of time, but for a trip whose value is quiet, it can feel rushed.

**Four nights** feels far more natural. You get time on board, morning and evening light, slack at Abu Simbel, and time to take in how empty the lake is. For a first Lake Nasser trip, four nights is the right baseline.

**Five nights or more** is for special interests: fishing, birdwatching, photography, or a near-private, slower trip.

## Aswan or Abu Simbel: which direction

Itineraries either sail south from Aswan and finish at Abu Simbel, or start at Abu Simbel and sail north to Aswan.

**Southbound** builds toward a climax. You begin with the small temples and finish at Ramesses II's enormous façade, which makes for a clear story.

**Northbound** gives you Abu Simbel first, then lets you settle into the quiet of the lake. Ending in Aswan also makes it easier to continue to Philae, the Nubian Museum and Luxor.

Neither is better in itself. Decide by the rest of your trip. Fly into Aswan from Cairo and out of Abu Simbel at the end, or start at Abu Simbel, finish in Aswan, and join a regular Nile cruise. Choose whichever makes the transfers flow.

## Time at Abu Simbel

How long a cruise gives you at Abu Simbel is one of the most important things to compare.

A good itinerary does not rush it. The best pattern is seeing the façade in the afternoon or evening light, then going back the next morning. Abu Simbel early, before the day-trippers arrive from Aswan, makes a completely different impression from Abu Simbel at midday with the crowds.

Departures timed for the solar alignment around 22 February and 22 October are special, but crowded and more expensive. Even without the alignment, an early-morning visit is worth a lot.

Check whether the cruise treats Abu Simbel as a stop on the way or as the high point of the trip.

## The boat and the guide

Lake Nasser has far fewer boats than the Luxor–Aswan route, so the name of the boat tells you less than you might think. Confirm that the boat is actually operating on your dates, when it was last refurbished, how the cabins are cooled, what the food is like, and whether the deck is comfortable with enough shade.

Because the boat is where you spend most of your time, the details matter more here than on a normal Nile cruise. Can you see the water from your cabin? Does the air conditioning in the shared areas work well? Will the food suit you? Do not count on Wi-Fi. If you need it, ask.

The guide matters most of all. These temples do not explain themselves. Why they were moved, which period each building comes from, what the Christian traces mean, how it all ties into Nubian history: without that, the lake becomes a scatter of small ruins in an empty landscape. The quality of the guiding will change your trip more than the luxury of the boat.

## Combining it with a Nile cruise

You can combine Lake Nasser with a regular Nile cruise, but it makes a long trip on the water. A Luxor–Aswan cruise followed by Lake Nasser is a very slow journey. It suits people with time to spare who love temples and river travel.

On a shorter trip, choose one. On a first visit to Egypt, Cairo, Giza, Luxor, Aswan and a regular Nile cruise usually come first. For returning travellers, or anyone drawn to Abu Simbel and Nubia, adding Lake Nasser means a great deal. It is less the centre of an Egypt trip than a deep extra chapter.

## Prepare in Aswan

Most Lake Nasser trips start in or pass through Aswan, and a day there makes the lake far easier to read.

**The Nubian Museum** is the essential stop. Nubian history and culture, the displacement and the rescue campaign are all set out here. If you see it before you board, you will understand much more of what you see on the lake.

**Philae** is part of the same rescue story. It is not on the lake route, but it shows you what moving a temple to save it looks like.

**The Unfinished Obelisk** explains Aswan's granite and how ancient builders cut it. A Lake Nasser cruise is a trip on water, but behind it is a long history of cutting, moving and re-erecting stone.

## Season and what to pack

Lake Nasser works best from October to April. Winter days are comfortable, with cool mornings and evenings, and the temples are easy to walk. December to February is peak demand, so book good boats and good dates early.

Summer is very hot. The boat is air-conditioned, but the temples are outdoors, and July and August on the lake are hard even for people who handle heat well. If you are considering summer, base the decision on how much outdoor sightseeing you can actually tolerate, not just on price or availability.

The February and October solar-alignment weeks are special, but crowds and prices go up. If quiet is what you came for, missing those dates by a few days is a reasonable choice.

Pack a hat, sunglasses, sunscreen, a light long-sleeved shirt and good walking shoes. The sun is strong on the lake and reflects off the water. Mornings and evenings can be cool depending on the season, so bring a thin extra layer.

## What to confirm before booking

- Departure date, and that the departure is confirmed to operate
- Boarding and disembarkation points
- Arrival time at Abu Simbel, and how long you have there
- Visit time at each temple
- Guide's language and quality
- Whether entrance fees are included
- Whether transfers from Aswan or Abu Simbel airport are included
- How tipping is handled
- Cancellation terms

Lake Nasser has few boats and is not a market for last-minute flexibility. In winter, and especially around the solar-alignment weeks, booking early is the safer choice. Do not judge a boat on old reviews alone. Refurbishments, operators and itineraries change, so check the current situation when you book.

## Who it suits, and who it doesn't

A Lake Nasser cruise is not a trip every first-time visitor needs. It sits a little off Egypt's main route.

It suits people who have already seen the essentials, who want to see Abu Simbel from the water, who are interested in Nubian history, and who like seeing sites slowly in a quiet landscape. It suits people who like places with few people and who enjoy time on board doing nothing much.

It does not suit people who want to cover the major sites quickly on a short schedule, who want town walks, nightlife and shopping, or who do not do well with long stretches on a boat.

## In sum

Lake Nasser is not Egypt's glamorous front street. It is wide water, desert cliffs, rescued temples, and the memory of drowned Nubia. The trip is quiet, slow, and at times a little lonely. Whether you find that loneliness beautiful decides whether the trip is for you.

When you choose a cruise, look past the star rating. Check how many nights, how much time you get at Abu Simbel, whether the temple guiding is good, and whether the cruise links naturally with the rest of your itinerary.

This trip is not an Egypt essential. But it shows you something the main route does not: that Egypt is not only a country of ancient sites, but a country that made hard modern choices and still carries their cost. For the travellers it suits, it becomes one of the most memorable parts of an Egypt trip.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a Lake Nasser cruise worth it on a first trip to Egypt?

Usually not as the main cruise. On a first trip, Cairo, Giza, Luxor, Aswan and a regular Luxor–Aswan Nile cruise give you more of Egypt's essentials. Lake Nasser is worth it on a first trip only if Abu Simbel and Nubian history are high on your list and you have the extra days. Otherwise it makes an excellent second trip.

### How many nights should a Lake Nasser cruise be?

Four nights is the right baseline. Three nights works if you are short of time, but it feels rushed on a trip whose value is quiet. Five nights or more suits fishing, birdwatching, photography or a slower, near-private trip.

### Is it better to start in Aswan or at Abu Simbel?

Neither is better in itself. Sailing south from Aswan builds toward Abu Simbel as the finale. Sailing north from Abu Simbel ends in Aswan, which makes it easy to continue to Philae, the Nubian Museum, Luxor or a regular Nile cruise. Choose the direction that fits your flights and the rest of your itinerary.

### When is the best time for a Lake Nasser cruise?

October to April. Winter days are comfortable for walking the temples, and December to February is the busiest period. July and August are very hot for outdoor visits. The weeks around 22 February and 22 October, when the sun reaches the inner sanctuary at Abu Simbel, are special but crowded and more expensive.

### How far ahead should I book a Lake Nasser cruise?

Earlier than for a regular Nile cruise. Only a few boats operate on the lake, so the good dates go first, especially from December to February and around the solar-alignment weeks. For winter travel, start a few months ahead, and confirm that your departure is guaranteed to operate.
