# Travel2Egypt Concierge — Operating Principles for the Team

*A condensed guide for staff handling briefs that arrive from the AI concierge.*

*Reading time: 15 minutes. Required before handling any incoming brief.*

---

## What this document is and why it matters

The AI concierge produces briefs based on a specific way of treating travelers. That way is shaped by 30 years of how Travel2Egypt actually operates. When you respond to a brief, your follow-up email should feel like the natural next step from the conversation the visitor just had — not a fresh start, not a different voice, not a different set of promises.

Visitors will notice if the AI treats them one way and the human team treats them another way. They will not say it out loud, but they will feel it, and it erodes trust in the most fragile moment of the customer journey: the handoff from chat to proposal.

This document captures the few things that matter most about how the AI behaves, so you can mirror those patterns in your follow-up. It is not a script. It is a set of operating principles to internalize and adapt.

---

## The core idea

We are not designing trips. We are aligning expectations with reality.

If a traveler's expectations are wrong, the perfect itinerary will feel disappointing. If their expectations are right, even small imperfections will be forgiven.

This is the orientation of the AI concierge and it should be the orientation of every follow-up email. Your job is not to sell a trip. Your job is to translate what the traveler wants into the Egypt experience that will actually make them happy. Sometimes that means gentle pushback. Sometimes it means recommending less than they asked for. Sometimes it means redirecting them to AffordEgypt because Travel2Egypt is not the right fit. Every one of these moves builds long-term trust even when it does not produce a short-term booking.

## How to read a brief

The brief that lands in your inbox (or in Autoura) contains structured information plus a transcript and a summary. Read in this order:

1. **The brief summary first.** This is one paragraph in the AI's voice describing the traveler. It captures the essence of what they want and why. Read it carefully — it tells you the emotional shape of the trip.

2. **The structured fields second.** Number of travelers, dates, destinations, comfort level, dietary needs. This is the operational layer.

3. **The transcript third, scanning for nuance.** What did they push back on? What did they get excited about? What did the AI promise on our behalf? What concerns did they raise? The transcript is where you find the texture that the structured fields cannot hold.

Do not skip the transcript. The structured fields are necessary but never sufficient. The transcript is where the relationship was built.

## The promises the AI makes on your behalf

The AI commits to specific things in conversation. You inherit these commitments. Honor them.

**Response timing.** The AI tells visitors that briefs received before 1 p.m. Cairo time are answered the same day by 8 p.m. Cairo time. After 1 p.m., the response goes out by 10 a.m. Cairo time the next morning. This holds seven days a week, including weekends and holidays.

This is the most important commitment. If you are on rotation and a brief lands in your queue, you are responsible for getting a response out within the window. If you cannot, hand it off explicitly to whoever is next on rotation. Do not let it sit.

If something genuinely prevents the response — system outage, internet failure, emergency — Islam needs to know immediately so he can adjust the team's coverage.

**Quality over speed.** Within the window, take the time to respond properly. A thoughtful response at 7:55 p.m. is better than a rushed response at 4 p.m. The visitor will remember the quality long after they have forgotten the exact time.

**Proposal with specific pricing.** The follow-up email is the formal proposal. It includes specific pricing, specific recommendations, specific options where appropriate. Not "starting from" — actual numbers for what we are recommending. The AI deferred pricing to you; deliver it.

**Personal contact.** The visitor knows they were talking to AI. They expect the human follow-up to feel personal. Sign your email with your name, not a generic "Travel2Egypt team." Include a direct line of contact (your email, your WhatsApp number) so they know who they are dealing with.

## The voice we use

The AI uses a specific voice. Match it.

**Direct, not effusive.** "I have reviewed your brief and put together two options for you" beats "Thank you so much for your wonderful inquiry, we are absolutely thrilled to help you plan your dream Egyptian adventure." The visitor wants a competent advisor, not a marketing pitch.

**Specific, not generic.** "The Old Cataract terrace tea at sunset" beats "luxurious dining experiences." Operational specifics signal expertise. Generic luxury language signals everyone else.

**Honest, not salesy.** If something they want will not work well, say so. If we recommend against an option, explain why in plain language. The visitor came to us because they want a real opinion, not a yes machine.

**Warm, but disciplined.** Egyptian hospitality is real, and you can let it show. But long flowery passages erode the directness the visitor expects. If a sentence reads just as strongly without "amazing" or "incredible," remove the adjective.

**No marketing filler.** Avoid: "stunning," "breathtaking," "once-in-a-lifetime," "unforgettable journey," "majestic," "iconic," "must-see." These words were once specific. Now they are noise. Replace them with concrete details: "the late afternoon light on the West Bank tombs" beats "stunning views of ancient wonders."

**No "honestly" or "to be honest" as filler.** Same principle as the AI follows. These phrases erode trust when overused. Be honest without announcing it.

**No "let me see what I can do."** This phrase invites negotiation downward and damages trust. If a visitor pushes for a discount, hold price with confidence, redirect to AffordEgypt if their budget is genuinely incompatible, or politely decline. Never enter price-cutting spirals.

## Pricing — what to quote, in what currency, with what flexibility

**Default currency: Euro.** All quotes in EUR. Visitors may pay in EUR, USD, GBP, or EGP, but our reference currency is Euro.

**Two-tier deposit structure:**

- **Light services** (day tours, 1-2 day trips, transportation only): 10% deposit at booking via Tab.travel link in EUR. Balance on arrival, payable by card via Tab.travel terminal in EUR or by POS terminal in EGP. Cash also accepted in EUR, USD, GBP, or EGP.

- **Tour packages** (multi-day comprehensive bookings): 25% deposit at first booking via Tab.travel link in EUR. Subsequent payment terms designed tour by tour at proposal completion — discuss with the visitor what works for them.

**Confirm which category applies before quoting deposit terms.** Quoting 10% on a 14-day cruise package is wrong and will need correction.

**Tab.travel, not PayPal.** We use Tab.travel for card payments. It is purpose-built for travel agencies and carries refund and dispute mechanisms appropriate to travel bookings. Do not reference PayPal.

**Pricing expectations from the AI conversation.** The AI may have given the visitor a rough order of magnitude during the chat — for example, "a trip of this shape would typically sit around €1,500 per person." Your formal quote should be near this range. If the actual quote is significantly above or below the AI's rough range, address it explicitly: "The rough range you saw earlier was €1,500. Once we costed the specific arrangements (private dahabiya for late October dates, the boutique guide profile you described), the actual figure is €X. The increase is largely the dahabiya tier." Visitors handle clarity well; surprises feel like bait-and-switch.

**Egyptian inflation as honest context.** If the visitor expected lower prices based on older information, you can acknowledge it: "Egypt has been through significant inflation in 2024-2026 and pricing has moved with it. What you may have read from a year or two ago does not reflect current realities." Not as an excuse — as honest context.

## The trust-collapse moment around payment

This is the most important pattern in our customer interactions. You will see it.

The visitor has gone through the conversation, received the proposal, and is about to commit. Then they hesitate. They write something like: "I cannot find reviews of you online" or "you are not on social media much" or "how do I know this is real?"

This is not rejection. It is a request for permission to trust you. Handle it directly, never defensively.

**The right response, in this exact emotional shape:**

1. Acknowledge the concern as legitimate. Do not push back on it. Do not be wounded by it.

2. Direct them to TripAdvisor where reviews live (`https://www.tripadvisor.com/Attraction_Review-g294201-d17406742-Reviews-Travel2Egypt-Cairo_Cairo_Governorate.html`).

3. Explain that payment is processed through Tab.travel — a payment platform designed specifically for travel agencies, with refund and dispute mechanisms appropriate to travel bookings.

4. Offer the deposit-only flexibility: small percentage to confirm, balance on arrival.

5. **And here is the move that converts:** if they remain uncomfortable, tell them honestly that they should book with a company they trust. Do not push. Do not beg.

The visitor who feels respected when raising the concern often books. The visitor who feels pressured disappears.

A specific phrase Islam has used many times: *"Take your time. If after looking around you decide we are right for you, we will be here. If you decide a different company suits you better, that is the right choice for you and I wish you a wonderful trip."*

Try this phrasing. It rarely loses bookings. It often confirms them.

## The AffordEgypt redirect

Travel2Egypt operates at a specific quality standard with a corresponding price. Some visitors are not the right fit for our pricing. They are still good people who deserve a good Egypt experience — but at a different operator.

**AffordEgypt.com is our sister brand for these visitors.** Same destinations, lower price points, with appropriate trade-offs in vehicle category, hotel tier, and group arrangement.

**When to redirect:**

- The visitor names a budget that is genuinely incompatible with our standard
- The visitor pushes hard for a 30%+ discount
- The visitor's expectations on group size, vehicle, or hotel tier consistently push toward the budget end
- You can tell from the conversation they are not going to be happy at our prices

**How to redirect:**

Do not diminish their choice. Do not imply they are cheap. Frame it as a fit issue:

*"Travel2Egypt operates at a specific quality standard that has a price. From what you have shared, our sister brand AffordEgypt.com may suit you better — same destinations, lower price points, appropriate trade-offs. They have served thousands of travelers happily. Here is the link if you would like to start that conversation: [link]. Either way, I wish you a wonderful trip."*

This redirect is one of the most strategic moves we make. It protects our quality standard while still serving travelers who reach out. They remember the kindness even if they book elsewhere, and many come back to Travel2Egypt for a future trip when their budget allows.

## When to push back on a brief

Sometimes the brief that arrives describes a trip that will not work well. The AI may have already pushed back in conversation, or the visitor may have insisted. Your follow-up is a chance to reframe with the formal weight of a proposal behind it.

**Common scenarios:**

**Too much in too few days.** "12 days, Cairo + Luxor + Aswan + Hurghada + Siwa + Dahab + Sharm." You cannot cover this in 12 days well. Respond with: "Your brief shows the destinations you are excited about. With 12 days, doing all of them would mean spending more time in airports than at the sites themselves. I have built two options: one that does six of these properly, and one that does eight of them at faster pace. My recommendation is the first. Here is why..."

**Wrong season for what they want.** Mid-July White Desert. Late October Mediterranean swimming. Tell them what the season actually delivers. Offer alternatives. Let them choose with full information.

**Hotel category mismatch.** Visitor wants luxury but their budget is mid-range, or vice versa. Address directly: "Based on your budget, here is what fits. If you would like to step up to the next tier, the additional cost is approximately €X per person and the difference is [specifics]."

**Religious or cultural assumptions.** Visitor planning trip during Ramadan without knowing what that means for restaurants, alcohol, hours. Religious traveler whose pilgrimage goals were not surfaced. Surface what was missed.

The push-back move requires care. It is not "you are wrong." It is "let me show you what you are likely to actually experience, and you decide." Treat the visitor as intelligent. Give them the information. Let them choose.

## Decline patterns — when not to take a booking

Some inquiries we should not accept. Quality calls, not profit calls. Examples:

- Aggressive discount demands (40-50% off). The original price was either dishonest or the discount is unsustainable. Either way, decline.
- Last-minute requests with no flexibility on dates, hotels, or vehicles when the requested arrangements are genuinely unavailable.
- Dismissive attitude toward local realities (insistence that Ramadan hours do not apply, that convoy schedules are negotiable, that road permissions are optional).
- Requests for venue rentals, event production, or other non-travel services. Not what we do.
- Destinations we do not cover well (Ethiopia, Lebanon, anything outside Egypt and Jordan).

**How to decline professionally:**

*"After reviewing your brief, I do not think Travel2Egypt is the right fit for what you are describing. [Specific reason in one sentence.] I would rather be honest about this than take the booking and risk disappointing you on a trip that matters this much. I wish you success in finding the right fit elsewhere."*

No moralizing. No over-apology. Clean closure.

## When something goes wrong on a trip

Briefs that successfully convert into trips occasionally produce issues. A flight is cancelled, a hotel overbooks, a guide does not show, a cruise itinerary changes. The visitor messages you in distress.

**The Travel2Egypt approach:**

1. Acknowledge the issue immediately. No defensiveness. No "but our policy is..."

2. Explain what is happening in plain language. If we caused the issue, say so. If the issue is upstream of us, explain that without dodging responsibility for resolution.

3. Offer remedy. Concrete and specific. "We will arrange [X] within [timeframe]." Not "we are looking into options."

4. Follow through. The visitor's stress decreases proportionally to the precision of the remedy. Vague reassurance increases stress.

5. After resolution, follow up. A short message a day or two later asking how they are doing closes the loop and frequently produces a positive review or a recommendation to other travelers.

Islam has handled hundreds of these moments. The pattern is consistent: visitors who experience a problem handled well end up more loyal than visitors whose trips go perfectly. Mistakes are an opportunity if you handle them with discipline.

## What to do if the AI got something wrong

The AI is not infallible. Occasionally it will say something inaccurate in a conversation. Common areas:

- A specific tour or experience that we do not actually offer
- A pricing range that does not reflect current rates
- An operational detail that has changed (a flight schedule, a road status, a hotel renovation)
- A nationality calibration that misread the visitor

**When you encounter this in a brief transcript:**

1. Address it directly in your response. Do not pretend it did not happen.

2. Correct the inaccuracy without throwing the AI under the bus. "I want to clarify something from our earlier conversation. The flight schedule for [destination] has actually changed and the option we discussed is no longer available. Here is what is available now."

3. Flag the conversation for review. Use the admin panel link in your incoming brief notification to mark it for Islam's daily review. He will use these to refine the prompt over time.

4. Do not make excuses for the AI. The visitor knows it is AI. They do not need an apology for AI behavior. They need accurate information from you now.

## When you receive a brief flagged with the escape hatch

If the visitor used "Talk to a human" during the conversation, the brief arrives flagged with this fact. Treat it as elevated priority.

The visitor may have used the escape hatch because:

- The AI failed them in some way (factually, emotionally, or operationally)
- They had a specific concern they wanted a human to address
- They are sophisticated enough to want human review of a complex inquiry

**Your follow-up should:**

1. Acknowledge directly that they wanted to speak with a human. "I see you asked to speak with our team directly — happy to take this from here."

2. Read the transcript carefully to identify what triggered the escape. If you can address it explicitly, do.

3. Be slightly more personal in tone than a standard brief response. The visitor sought human contact; deliver it.

4. Make it easy for them to continue the conversation directly with you, not back through the AI. Include your direct WhatsApp or phone number prominently.

## Coordination across the rotation

You are part of a rotation of three or four people handling incoming briefs. The visitor experience needs to feel like one team, not four different voices.

**Patterns that help:**

- Use a shared template structure (provided in the templates document) so every response has consistent organization. Customize the content; keep the structure.
- Sign your name at the bottom but identify yourself as "from the Travel2Egypt team" in the body. Visitors should know they are dealing with a real person while feeling part of a coherent operation.
- When handing off mid-conversation (e.g., a brief lands in your queue but you are about to be off-shift), do so explicitly with the visitor: "I am Sara from the Travel2Egypt team. My colleague Ahmed will be your point of contact from here — copying him on this email."
- Read the previous responses in any ongoing thread before replying. Continuity matters.
- When a question is ambiguous, ask within the team Slack/WhatsApp before responding to the visitor. Better to delay a response by 30 minutes than to say something that contradicts what someone else has said.

**Patterns that hurt:**

- Generic greetings that read like form letters
- Inconsistent positioning (one team member quotes higher, another lower; one is formal, another casual)
- Responding without reading the brief or transcript
- Ignoring concerns the visitor raised in chat that you would have addressed if you had read the conversation
